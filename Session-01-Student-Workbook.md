## Part 2 · Theory — statistical studies, variables and one-variable tables

Read all of this before Part 3. Each subsection gives the rule, the course-description code it comes from, what the rule does **not** say, and one tiny made-up illustration. The questions at the end check that it went in.

### The four statistical practices: where today sits

The course framework organises everything into four statistical practices:

> **Formulate Questions · Collect Data · Analyze Data · Interpret Results**

Every topic between now and the exam is a tool used at one of those four stages, and each skill code names its practice by its first digit: 1.A formulates a question, 2.A identifies information, 3.A constructs a table or graph, 4.A describes and compares. The exam is built on them: free-response Question 1 is a multi-focus question on Practices 1 and 2, and Question 2 on Practices 3 and 4.

Today is mostly the first two moves, with a first step into the last two when you build tables and check a claim. There is very little calculation. That can make this session feel like it doesn't count — it does. If you cannot say precisely what your population is, all the inference in Units 3 and 4 falls apart, and those two units are between a quarter and nearly half of the multiple-choice section.

### Statistical study, datum and data set (1.1.A.1–3)

**A statistical study collects data from a sample to answer an investigative question about a larger population** (1.1.A.1). Studies are needed because the population is too large, or too difficult to reach, for data to be collected from every item or individual in it (1.1.A.2).

A **datum** is one piece of information about one item or individual; *data* is the plural, and a collection of data is a **data set** (1.1.A.3).

What it does not say: a study is not defined by its calculations. A study with no arithmetic at all is still a statistical study if it uses a sample to answer a question about a population.

*Illustration.* "Box 7 weighs 1.2 kg" is one datum. The weights of all the boxes checked, together, are the data set.

### Population and sample: N and n (1.1.A.4–5)

The **population** is all the items or individuals of interest; its size is written **N** (1.1.A.4). The **sample** is the subset of the population from which data are actually obtained; the number in it is the **sample size**, written **n** (1.1.A.5).

Two things students get wrong. First, the population is fixed by the **question**, not by the data: it is whoever the question is about, whether or not anyone measured them. Second, the sample sits **inside** the population; it is a subset, not a separate group. When data are recorded from every member of the population there is no sample, and the study is a **census** (1.10.B.1; Session 6 returns to it).

N and n are sizes, not summaries of a variable, so neither is a parameter or a statistic.

*Illustration.* A factory wants to know about a delivery of 500 screws and tests 20 of them: N = 500 and n = 20. Had it tested all 500, that would be a census.

### Results in context (1.1.A.6)

Every component of a study, and every number calculated from it, corresponds to something in the real situation it came from. Naming that correspondence is what the course means by **in context** (1.1.A.6).

What it does not say: context is not decoration added at the end. On the exam a correct number without context usually earns nothing, because the number alone does not say what was measured, on whom, or in what units.

*Illustration.* If 3 of the 20 screws tested are faulty, the answer is not "0.15" but "15% of the 20 screws tested were faulty."

### The investigative question (1.1.B.1–2)

An **investigative question** is the question a study exists to answer. It must have a **defined purpose** and must **not be changed based on the data analysis or results** (1.1.B.1). It must be posed so that **the required data can be collected and analysed** (1.1.B.2).

Why the second half of 1.1.B.1 matters: in any data set some pattern looks interesting by chance. A question chosen after seeing that pattern would be answered by the very data that suggested it, so those data cannot fairly answer it. The new question is legitimate, for a **new** study with **new** data.

What it does not say: a question that data could inform is not automatically investigative. "Should we buy more?" is a **decision** and "How can we increase it?" is an **action**; a study can inform both but answers neither. A question that presupposes its own conclusion ("Why is it so bad?") assumes a fact nobody has established.

*Illustration.* "Is this bread good?" names nothing measurable. "What proportion of this bakery's loaves weigh less than 400 g?" names a population, a variable, and data that can be collected.

### Observational units and variables (1.2.A.1–3)

An **observational unit** is the item or individual from which a datum is collected (1.2.A.1). A **variable** is a characteristic that may change from one observational unit to another (1.2.A.2). Picture the data set as a spreadsheet: one row per observational unit, one column per variable.

What it does not say: the unit is not whatever a variable happens to describe. If each person reports how long their journey took, the unit is the person, not the journey. Something that is the same for every unit in a study, such as the name of the one school involved, is not a variable in that study. And data need not be numbers or words: photographs, sounds, videos and text recorded on units can all carry meaningful information (1.2.A.3).

*Illustration.* In the screw check, one row is one screw; its length, its mass and whether it is faulty are three variables.

### Parameters and statistics (1.2.A.4–5)

A **parameter** is a numerical summary of the variable of interest for a **population** (1.2.A.4). A **statistic** is a numerical summary of the variable of interest for a **sample** (1.2.A.5). The value of a statistic from a particular sample is often **not equal** to the unknown value of the parameter, but it can be the **basis for inferences** about it (1.2.A.5).

The numerical summaries this session uses are a **proportion**, a count divided by the total it is out of, and a **mean**, the ordinary average (Session 3 defines it formally).

How to tell which is which: ask **of whom** the summary was computed, which is the same as asking what the denominator counts. Computed from the units actually measured, and they are a subset: a statistic, and known. Describing everyone of interest: a parameter, and usually unknown, because nobody measured everyone.

What it does not say: "the mean" and "the proportion" are neither until you know whose they are. A mean computed from a census is a parameter.

*Illustration.* A jar holds 1,000 beads and nobody has counted the red ones. The proportion of red among all 1,000 is a parameter. Draw 50 and find 13 red: 13 ÷ 50 = 0.26 is a statistic. A second draw of 50 would probably give a different statistic; the parameter would not have changed.

### Categorical and quantitative variables (1.2.B.1–2)

A **categorical** variable, also called **qualitative**, takes values that are **category names or group labels** (1.2.B.1). A **quantitative** variable, also called **numerical**, takes **numerical values for a measured or counted quantity** and generally has **units of measure** (1.2.B.2).

The misreading: "it is written in digits, so it is quantitative." Digits can be a label. Two questions settle it, and together they are the **digits test**: does the value have **units of measure**, and does **arithmetic on it mean anything**? If not, the variable is categorical however it is written. Coding yes and no as 1 and 0 does not make a variable quantitative either.

*Illustration.* Bus route 42 has no units, and route 42 is not twice route 21: categorical. A journey of 42 km has units, and it is twice 21 km: quantitative.

### Discrete and continuous quantitative variables (1.2.C.1–2)

A **discrete** quantitative variable can take a **countable** number of values, which may be finite or countably infinite, like the whole numbers (1.2.C.1). A **continuous** quantitative variable can take **infinitely many values within an interval**, including every value between any two of them, so its values are measurable but not countable (1.2.C.2). Rule of thumb: you **count** discrete things and **measure** continuous ones.

What it does not say: discrete or continuous describes the values the variable can take, not the values of its summaries. A discrete variable can have a mean of 1.4 even though no unit has 1.4 of anything. And a continuous variable recorded to the nearest minute is still continuous: the rounding is in the recording, not in the quantity.

*Illustration.* The number of pets in a home is 0, 1, 2, …, never 1.5: discrete. A pet's mass can be 3.2 kg, 3.25 kg, or anything between: continuous.

### Frequency and relative frequency tables (1.3.A.1–2)

A **frequency table** shows the number of observational units in each category of a categorical variable (1.3.A.1). A **relative frequency table** shows the **proportion** of observational units in each category (1.3.A.2).

:::formula
rf = f ÷ n  ·  100rf = (f ÷ n) × 100
:::

*f* is the frequency, the count in one category; *n* is the total number of observational units in the table, which for a survey is the sample size; *rf* is the relative frequency; *100rf* is the same proportion written as a percentage. Every one is a division, and the difficulty is knowing which number goes underneath: it is always *n*, the total of the whole table, never another category's count.

Three self-checks catch nearly every arithmetic slip:

:::checks
Σf = n
Σrf = 1
Σ100rf = 100%
:::

What it does not say: a relative frequency is a share of **this** total. Change the total, for example by dropping a category, and every remaining relative frequency changes, so no category is "too small to matter". Rounded values may add to 0.999 or 1.001; a larger gap is an error, not rounding.

*Illustration.* Ten answers: A, B, A, C, A, B, A, C, A, B. Frequencies A 5, B 3, C 2, and Σf = 10. Relative frequencies 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, and Σrf = 1. Percentages 50%, 30%, 20%.

### Proportions, percentages and ratios in a claim (1.3.B.1–2)

Percentages, relative frequencies and ratios all give **the same information as proportions** (1.3.B.1). Counts and relative frequencies reveal information that can be used to **justify claims** about the variable **in context** (1.3.B.2).

A justified claim has four parts, and they are the template for the whole course: **state the claim · quote the number · say what the number does and does not establish · keep it in context.**

Two misreadings cost marks all year. **"Most" means more than half**: the largest category is the most common one, but "most units are in it" is true only if its share is above 50%, and exactly 50% is half, not most. And **percentages of the same total are added, never averaged**: the share in "either of two categories" is the sum of their two shares.

*Illustration.* 3 out of 12 is a proportion of 0.25, a relative frequency of 0.25, 25%, "1 in 4", or a ratio of 1 : 3 of yes to no: one fact, five forms. In a table with shares of 40%, 35% and 25%, the 40% category is the most common, but "most are in it" is false; the other two together are 35% + 25% = 60%.

### Check yourself before the commute study

Six quick questions. Answer each on paper, then open its reveal. If one goes wrong, reread the subsection it names before you start Part 3.

**Check 1.** A company surveys 80 of its 600 employees. What are N and n, and is 600 a parameter?

:::reveal Reveal — check 1
N = 600 and n = 80. **Neither is a parameter**: both are sizes, not summaries of a variable. (*Population and sample*.)
:::

**Check 2.** The proportion of those 80 who work from home on Fridays: parameter or statistic?

:::reveal Reveal — check 2
A **statistic**, because it was computed from the sample of 80, not from all 600. (*Parameters and statistics*.)
:::

**Check 3.** An employee ID number: categorical or quantitative?

:::reveal Reveal — check 3
**Categorical.** It has no units, and averaging ID numbers means nothing. (*Categorical and quantitative variables*, the digits test.)
:::

**Check 4.** Number of meetings attended last week; time spent in meetings. Discrete or continuous?

:::reveal Reveal — check 4
Meetings: **discrete**, you count them. Time: **continuous**, you measure it. (*Discrete and continuous quantitative variables*.)
:::

**Check 5.** One category of a relative frequency table is 45%. Can you say most units are in it?

:::reveal Reveal — check 5
**No.** "Most" means more than half, and 45% is less than half. (*Proportions, percentages and ratios in a claim*.)
:::

**Check 6.** After seeing the results, the manager changes the survey's question to fit a pattern she spotted. What rule has she broken?

:::reveal Reveal — check 6
An investigative question should **not be changed based on the data analysis or results** (1.1.B.1). Her new question needs a new study with new data. (*The investigative question*.)
:::

## Part 3 — The commute study

Read this twice before you go on. Everything below applies Part 2; nothing new is introduced.

> Lincoln High School has **1,842 students**. The principal wants to reorganise the morning bus timetable but does not know how students currently get to school. She asks a randomly chosen **120 students** to record, on one Tuesday: how they primarily travelled to school, how many minutes the journey took, and how many separate vehicles they had to change between.
>
> Of the 120 surveyed, **43 came by bus**, and their journeys averaged **24.6 minutes**.

### Who is the study about?

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Write down: who is this study actually about? All 1,842 students, or the 120 who answered? Give N and n.
:::

:::reveal
All **1,842**. Ask yourself whether the principal would be satisfied knowing only about those 120 — of course not. She has to run a timetable for the whole school.

This is *Population and sample* from Part 2, applied: the **population** is all 1,842 Lincoln High students, so **N = 1,842**; the **sample** is the 120 surveyed, so **n = 120**.

Notice the population is decided by **the question**, not by the data. The principal is not trying to say anything about students at other schools, so they are not in the population.
:::

### What is one row of the data?

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Imagine the results as a spreadsheet. What does one row represent?
:::

:::reveal
**One student.** This is the question that catches people out — plenty of people answer "one journey" or "one bus".

The **observational unit** is the individual each datum is collected *from* (*Observational units and variables*), so one row is one student. The data are not about journeys and not about buses. They are about students, each of whom happens to have a journey.
:::

### Sorting the variables

Three things were measured on each student: **mode of travel**, **journey time in minutes**, and **number of vehicle changes**.

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Sort them into "numbers" and "not numbers". Then look again at the two that are numbers — is there any difference between them? Could someone change 1.4 vehicles? Could a journey take 24.61 minutes?
:::

:::reveal
Apply the two tests from Part 2 in order: are the values labels or quantities (*Categorical and quantitative variables*), and if quantities, are they counted or measured (*Discrete and continuous quantitative variables*)?

| Variable | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mode of travel | Categorical | Bus, car, walk, bike are group labels |
| Journey time | Quantitative, **continuous** | Measured in minutes; 24.61 is possible |
| Number of vehicle changes | Quantitative, **discrete** | Counted; you change 0, 1 or 2, never 1.4 |
:::

![Decision tree for classifying a variable](figures/variable-types.svg)

*Keep this. It is the same two tests as a decision tree, the fastest way to classify any variable you meet, and the box on the left is the trap that Part 5 is built around.*

### The centre of the whole course

This next bit is the most important thing in the session. Take it slowly.

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Two questions, and they are not the same question.

1. What proportion of **all 1,842** students come to school by bus?
2. What proportion of **the 120 surveyed** came to school by bus?

Then say which answer is a parameter and which a statistic.
:::

:::reveal
1. **We don't know.** Nobody asked all 1,842. If you wrote 0.358, ask yourself: how many of the 1,842 did she actually ask?
2. **43 ÷ 120 = 0.358.** We know this exactly, because we computed it from data we have.

This is *Parameters and statistics* from Part 2: the first is a **parameter**, a summary for the population, and unknown; the second is a **statistic**, a summary for the sample, and known because you computed it.

| Quantity | Which | Do we know it? |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion of **all 1,842** who come by bus | Parameter | No |
| 43 ÷ 120 = 0.358, proportion of the **sampled** students | Statistic | Yes |
| Mean journey time of **all 1,842** | Parameter | No |
| 24.6 minutes, the mean for the **120** | Statistic | Yes |

**Every single thing you do from Session 13 onwards is about the gap between those two columns.** That is the subject. Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, p-values — all of it is machinery for saying something responsible about the left column when all you have is the right one.

**The memory hook:** **P**opulation goes with **P**arameter. **S**ample goes with **S**tatistic.
:::

![A sample inside a population, a statistic estimating an unknown parameter](figures/study-structure.svg)

*This one picture is most of AP Statistics. The sample sits **inside** the population — it is a subset, not a separate group. You measure the sample, compute a statistic, and use it to estimate a parameter you will never actually see.*

:::sim unit1-explorer | Unit 1 Explorer: Sample & Estimate | 760
The true proportion is fixed. Before each draw, predict whether the sample proportion will land exactly on it. Draw ten samples and count how often it does. Then write one sentence: why does the statistic change from sample to sample when the parameter never does?
:::

### The cyclists: changing the question after the data

Recall the rule from *The investigative question* in Part 2: a good investigative question has a defined purpose, can be answered with data you are able to collect, and — this is the part people miss — **is not changed after you see the results**.

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Here is the trap. Read it and commit to an answer before revealing.

> The principal looks at her results and notices that cyclists have much longer journeys than she expected. She's curious, so she rewrites her report to answer: *"Why do cyclists take so long?"*
>
> Is that allowed?
:::

:::reveal
**No** — and most people say yes, because it sounds like good scientific curiosity.

Two things are wrong with it:

1. **How many cyclists were there?** Out of 120 students, perhaps four. A pattern in four people is very weak evidence.
2. **She only asked the question because she spotted the pattern.** If she had gone looking, she could probably have found *some* odd-looking pattern in any of the three variables. Choosing your question after seeing which pattern looks interesting is how you end up "discovering" things that are just noise.

This is CED requirement **1.1.B.1**: an investigative question should not be changed based on the data analysis or results.

It is a perfectly good question — for a **new** study, collecting **new** data, designed to answer it.
:::

## Part 4 — Six studies: hold your ground

Your tutor does this by arguing with you. On your own, the argument is printed.

For each study: write down the **population**, the **sample**, the **observational unit**, and **one variable with its type**. Then read the challenge — someone disagreeing with you — and write a one-sentence rebuttal **before** you reveal the resolution.

If the challenge makes you change your mind, that is useful information: it means your first answer was a guess, not a reason.

### Study 1 — The supermarket checkout: who is the population?

:::yourturn
*A supermarket chain wants to know how long its customers wait at the checkout. Staff time 200 randomly chosen customers one Saturday.*

**Challenge:** *"The population is the 200 customers they timed — those are the people the study is about."*
:::

:::reveal
Population: all the chain's customers. Sample: the 200 timed. Unit: one customer. Variable: waiting time in minutes — quantitative, continuous.

The challenge confuses the sample with the population. The 200 are who they *measured*; the chain's customers generally are who they want to *know about*. If the 200 were the population, there would be nothing left to estimate and no reason to sample.
:::

### Study 2 — The phone batteries: what is the unit?

:::yourturn
*A phone manufacturer tests 500 randomly selected batteries from a production run of 80,000, to estimate how long the run's batteries last.*

**Challenge:** *"The observational unit is the production run."*
:::

:::reveal
Population: all 80,000 batteries in the run, N = 80,000. Sample: the 500 tested, n = 500. Unit: **one battery**. Variable: lifetime in hours — quantitative, continuous.

The production run is the population, not the unit. The unit is whatever a single measurement is taken from — and you measure the lifetime of one battery at a time.
:::

### Study 3 — The zoo tortoises: studied is not the same as of interest

:::yourturn
*A researcher wants to know the average lifespan of a species of tortoise. She studies the 23 tortoises living at one zoo.*

**Challenge:** *"The population is the 23 tortoises, because those are the ones she studied."*
:::

:::reveal
Population: all tortoises of that species. Sample: the 23 at the zoo. Unit: one tortoise. Variable: lifespan in years — quantitative, continuous.

Again, what she *studied* is the sample. What she wants to *know about* is the species — that is the population, and it is much larger than 23.

Worth noticing for later: 23 tortoises at a single zoo may be a poor stand-in for the whole species, since zoo animals are fed and protected. The sample is still a sample; it just may not be a good one. Session 6 is about exactly that.
:::

### Study 4 — The bus-route survey: is a 1/0 code quantitative?

:::yourturn
*A city surveys 600 of its 140,000 residents about a proposed bus route, recording for each whether they support it. Support is coded 1 for yes and 0 for no.*

**Challenge:** *"Support is recorded as 1 and 0, so it's a quantitative variable."*
:::

:::reveal
Population: all 140,000 residents, N = 140,000. Sample: the 600 surveyed, n = 600. Unit: one resident. Variable: whether they support the route — **categorical**.

The 1 and the 0 are labels standing in for "yes" and "no". They have no units, and arithmetic on them is not measuring anything — "the average support is 0.62" only means something because you have secretly converted it into *a proportion of yeses*, which is a categorical summary.

Coding a category as a digit does not turn it into a quantity: this is the digits test from Part 2. Hold this thought for Part 5.
:::

### Study 5 — The lightbulbs: census or sample?

:::yourturn
*A researcher tests every lightbulb coming off one production line during one shift, to judge the quality of the factory's output.*

**Challenge:** *"This isn't a sample at all — she tested every single one."*
:::

:::reveal
**It depends on the investigative question**, and that is the whole point of this card.

- If the question is *"how good was this shift's output?"* — she measured every item of interest. That is a **census**, not a sample, and her figures are **parameters**.
- If the question is *"how good is this factory's output in general?"* — then one shift is a **sample** of the factory's ongoing production, and her figures are **statistics**.

**The population is not a property of the data. It is a property of the question you are asking.** Two researchers with identical data can have different populations.
:::

### Study 6 — The teacher's 28 marks: is the mean a statistic?

:::yourturn
*A teacher records the marks of all 28 students in her class, to decide whether to re-teach a topic to that class.*

**Challenge:** *"She calculates the mean mark. That's a statistic."*
:::

:::reveal
Population: the 28 students in her class. Sample: **there isn't one** — she measured everybody. Unit: one student. Variable: mark — quantitative (discrete if marks are whole numbers).

Her mean is a **parameter**, not a statistic, because it summarises the entire population of interest.

This is the card worth remembering: **"the mean" is not automatically a statistic.** Whether a number is a parameter or a statistic depends entirely on whether you measured everybody or only some.
:::

## Part 5 — The hospital survey

> A regional hospital network discharged **12,400 patients** last year across six hospitals. To review its care, the network surveys **300** of those patients, recording for each: which hospital treated them, their room number, the number of nights they stayed, and the time in minutes from arrival to being seen by a doctor.
>
> Results by hospital: **St Mary's 84, Riverside 71, Northgate 52, Parkview 45, Eastwood 30, Hillcrest 18.**

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** You should be able to do these without help now. Population and N? Sample and n? Observational unit?
:::

:::reveal
Population: all 12,400 patients discharged last year, **N = 12,400**. Sample: the 300 surveyed, **n = 300**. Observational unit: one discharged patient.

If you needed to stop and think about these, go back and reread Part 3 before continuing.
:::

### The digits trap: room numbers

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Classify all four variables: hospital, room number, nights stayed, minutes until seen by a doctor.

Then, before revealing — answer this one honestly: **what is the average room number in this hospital?**
:::

:::reveal
If you tried to answer the room-number question, you probably realised you couldn't say what it would *mean*. Suppose it came out as 247.6. Is there a room 247.6? What would a manager do with that number?

| Variable | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital | Categorical | Six group labels |
| Room number | **Categorical** | Written with digits, but it is a label. No units of measure. The mean means nothing |
| Nights stayed | Quantitative, discrete | A count, with units. 5 nights is genuinely two more than 3 |
| Minutes until seen | Quantitative, continuous | Measured; 47.3 minutes is possible |

**The digits test from Part 2 — this is where it bites.** A variable written with digits is not automatically quantitative. Ask two questions:

> Does it have **units of measure**? Does **arithmetic on it mean anything**?

If the answer to both is no — ZIP code, room number, shirt number, phone number, the 1/0 coding from Study 4 — it is **categorical**.

If you answered "yes, it's a number" to Q4 back in Part 1, this is the correction. Go back and look at that question again now.
:::

### The frequency formulas, recalled for the hospital data

The formulas are in Part 2, under *Frequency and relative frequency tables*. Everything in this section is a division; the only difficulty is knowing which number goes underneath.

:::yourturn
**Your turn.** Without looking back, write the formulas for *rf* and *100rf*, and the three self-checks. For this survey, what number goes underneath?
:::

:::reveal
rf = f ÷ n and 100rf = (f ÷ n) × 100, where *f* is the count in one category and *n* is the total of the table: here **n = 300**, the patients surveyed. Not 12,400, and not another hospital's count.

The self-checks: Σ*f* = *n* · Σ*rf* = 1 · Σ*100rf* = 100%.
:::

:::note red Extension — not in the CED: the cumulative columns
Cumulative frequency is **not** examined (see the note at the end). It is here because Barron's uses it heavily and it makes "or fewer" questions immediate. Learn it for reading your prep book, not for the exam.

| Quantity | Symbol | Formula | In words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative frequency | *cf* | cf = running total of f | How many are in this category **or below** |
| Cumulative relative frequency | *crf* | crf = cf ÷ n | What fraction are in this category or below |
| Cumulative percentage | *100crf* | 100crf = (cf ÷ n) × 100 | The same thing, out of 100 |

**A note on notation.** Some textbooks write the last two as *100cf*. That is shorthand for "the cumulative figure expressed as a percentage" — it does **not** mean 100 × cf. The denominator is always *n*.

**Two more self-checks for cumulative columns:** the **last** *cf* = *n*, and the **last** *crf* = 1.
:::

# Reference sheet — keep this

Every term carries an example from this session. This is the page to revise from; in the session it is handed over at the end, not read aloud.

### Components of a study

**Statistical study** — data are collected from a sample to answer an investigative question about a larger population.
*Example: asking 120 of a school's 1,842 students how they travel, to learn how the whole school travels.*

**Datum · data · data set** — one piece of information about an individual; the plural; a collection of them.
*Example: "Maria walks to school" is one datum. All 120 completed survey forms together are the data set.*

**Population (N)** — all the items or individuals of interest.
*Example: all 1,842 students at Lincoln High, so N = 1,842.*

**Sample (n)** — the subset from which data are actually obtained.
*Example: the 120 students who filled in the survey, so n = 120.*

**In context** — connecting a result back to the real situation it came from. Every interpretation on the exam must be in context; a number alone never earns the point.
*Example: not "0.358", but "35.8% of the students surveyed travelled to school by bus."*

**Investigative question** — has a defined purpose, can be answered with data you can collect, and is not changed after you see the results.
*Example: good — "What proportion of Lincoln High students travel by bus?" Bad — "Is our school any good?", because nothing measurable would settle it.*

:::note amber An investigative question is not an action question
*"What proportion of members used the e-book service last year?"* is investigative — it names a measurable variable and data can settle it. *"Should we buy more e-books?"* is a **decision**, and *"How can we increase usage?"* is an **action**. A study can inform both, but it cannot answer either. Watch too for questions that **presuppose their own conclusion**: *"Why don't students read any more?"* assumes a fact that has not been established.
:::

### Describing what was measured

**Observational unit** — the individual a datum is collected *from*. One row of your spreadsheet.
*Example: one student. Not one journey, and not one bus.*

**Variable** — a characteristic that may change from one unit to another.
*Example: travel mode changes from student to student, so it is a variable. "School name" is the same in every row, so it is not.*

**Parameter** — a numerical summary for the **population**. Usually unknown.
*Example: the true proportion of all 1,842 students who take the bus. Nobody has measured it.*

**Statistic** — a numerical summary for the **sample**. Known, because you computed it.
*Example: 43 ÷ 120 = 0.358, the proportion of surveyed students who took the bus.*

**Sample size (n) and population size (N)** — facts about the study's design and the population's extent. **Neither is a parameter or a statistic**, because neither is a numerical summary of a variable.
*Example: in the commute study n = 120 and N = 1,842. Neither number tells you what proportion take the bus — that is the parameter, and it is still unknown.*

:::note teal Memory hook
**P**opulation → **P**arameter.  **S**ample → **S**tatistic.
:::

### Types of variable

**Categorical (qualitative)** — values are category names or group labels.
*Example: travel mode (bus, car, walk, bike); eye colour; a yes/no answer.*

**Quantitative (numerical)** — numerical values for a measured or counted quantity, generally with **units of measure**.
*Example: journey time in minutes; number of siblings; height in centimetres.*

**Discrete** — countable. Things you *count*.
*Example: number of vehicle changes — 0, 1, 2. Never 1.4.*

**Continuous** — any value in an interval. Things you *measure*.
*Example: journey time — 24 minutes, or 24.6, or 24.61.*

:::note amber The digits test
A variable written with digits is not automatically quantitative. Does it have **units**? Does **arithmetic on it mean anything**? If not — ZIP code, room number, shirt number, phone number, a 1/0 code for yes/no — it is categorical.
:::

:::note amber Discrete or continuous describes the variable, not its summaries
Number of vehicle changes is discrete, yet the mean number of changes could be 1.4. No student changed 1.4 vehicles — 1.4 is a property of the data set, not a value the variable can take. The same point returns in Unit 2, where a discrete random variable routinely has a non-whole expected value.
:::

### Frequency calculations

Let **n** be the sample size and **f** the count in one category.

| Quantity | Symbol | Formula | Example (f = 84, n = 300) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | *f* | the count itself | 84 |
| Relative frequency | *rf* | f ÷ n | 84 ÷ 300 = 0.280 |
| Percentage | *100rf* | (f ÷ n) × 100 | 0.280 × 100 = 28.0% |

**Self-checks:** Σ*f* = *n* · Σ*rf* = 1 · Σ*100rf* = 100%

**Frequency table** — the **count** in each category.
*Example: St Mary's 84, Riverside 71, … totalling 300.*

**Relative frequency table** — the **proportion** in each category. Must total 1.
*Example: St Mary's 0.280, Riverside 0.237, … totalling 1.000.*

:::note amber Percentages of the same total are added, never averaged
If espresso is 23.0% of orders and tea is 19.0%, together they are **42.0%** — not 21.0%. Averaging percentages is the commonest arithmetic error with these tables.
:::

:::note amber Relative frequency always depends on n
Drop a category and the total falls, so every remaining percentage rises. A 6% category is not "too small to matter" — removing it changes every other figure in the column.
:::

:::note red Extension, not examined — cumulative columns
| Quantity | Symbol | Formula | Example (nights stayed, n = 300) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative frequency | *cf* | running total of f | 96, then 96 + 84 = 180, … |
| Cumulative relative frequency | *crf* | cf ÷ n | 180 ÷ 300 = 0.600 |
| Cumulative percentage | *100crf* | (cf ÷ n) × 100 | 0.600 × 100 = 60.0% |

**Self-checks:** last *cf* = *n* · last *crf* = 1.

**Cumulative columns need ordered categories.** "This category or below" only means something if the categories line up. Nights stayed (1, 2, 3, 4, 5+) can be cumulated. Hospital names cannot — *"Riverside or fewer"* is not a sentence.
:::
