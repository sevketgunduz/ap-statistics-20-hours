## Before you start

None of the scenarios here is one you worked in Session 3. The commute times, the hospital waits, the library branches and the café have all been left out on purpose, because a familiar data set lets you remember an answer instead of working it out.

A calculator is allowed, as it is on the exam, but most questions need very little arithmetic. What they need is judgement: which summary to trust, what a boxplot can and cannot show, and when a number means something only against its own distribution.

**Use the course's quartile convention throughout.** Q1 is the median of the lower half of the ordered data and Q3 the median of the upper half. When *n* is odd, the overall median is left out of both halves. This is what a TI-84's 1-Var Stats does.

# The test

## Questions 1–6 · The animal shelter

> An animal shelter recorded how many days each of the **15 dogs** adopted last month had waited for a new home.

Waiting times in days, in order:

```
3   5   6   8   9  10  12  14
15  17  19  22  26  38  58
```

For these data, the mean is **17.5 days** and the standard deviation is ***s* = 14.4 days**.

**1.** The five-number summary of the waiting times is

- **(A)** 3, 8.5, 14, 20.5, 58
- **(B)** 3, 8, 14, 22, 58
- **(C)** 3, 8, 17.5, 22, 58
- **(D)** 3, 8, 14, 22, 43
- **(E)** 8, 14, 22, with no minimum or maximum given because 58 is an outlier

**2.** Using the 1.5 × IQR rule, which waiting times are outliers?

- **(A)** 38 and 58
- **(B)** None of them
- **(C)** 3 and 58
- **(D)** 58 only
- **(E)** 26, 38 and 58

**3.** A boxplot of these data is drawn with outliers shown separately. The right-hand whisker ends at

- **(A)** 38
- **(B)** 43
- **(C)** 58
- **(D)** 22
- **(E)** 36

**4.** At what percentile is a wait of **22 days**?

- **(A)** The 75th, because 22 is Q3
- **(B)** The 73rd, because 11 of the 15 waits are less than 22
- **(C)** The 80th, because 12 of the 15 waits are 22 days or less
- **(D)** The 12th, because 22 is the 12th value
- **(E)** The 22nd

**5.** A record is corrected: the dog that waited 58 days had in fact waited **158 days**. Which summaries change?

- **(A)** All of them, since one of the values has changed
- **(B)** The mean only
- **(C)** The mean and the median
- **(D)** The median and the IQR
- **(E)** The mean, the standard deviation and the range change. The median, the IQR and the 1.5 × IQR fences do not.

**6.** The shelter wants a single number for *"how long a typical dog waits"* on its website. Using the original data, which is the best choice, and why?

- **(A)** The mean, 17.5 days, because it uses every dog's waiting time
- **(B)** The median, 14 days, because the distribution is skewed right with a high outlier at 58, and the median is resistant to it
- **(C)** The mean, 17.5 days, because the mean is resistant to outliers
- **(D)** 30.5 days, halfway between the shortest and longest waits
- **(E)** Q3, 22 days, because most dogs have been adopted by then

## Questions 13–17 · Two pizza shops

> A student recorded the time, in minutes, from order to doorstep for **40 deliveries** from each of two pizza shops in the same town.

| Shop | *n* | Min | Q1 | Median | Q3 | Max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shop A | 40 | 12 | 18 | 22 | 28 | 41 |
| Shop B | 40 | 18 | 26 | 30 | 34 | 57 |

![Parallel boxplots of delivery times for Shop A and Shop B](figures/t03-boxplot-pizza.svg)

*Outliers are marked with an asterisk. Shop B's two outliers are at 51 and 57 minutes; its largest delivery time that is not an outlier is 42 minutes. Shop A has no outliers.*

**13.** Which answer would earn **full credit** for *"Compare the distributions of delivery times for the two shops"*?

- **(A)** "Shop A: median 22 minutes, IQR 10 minutes, skewed right, no outliers. Shop B: median 30 minutes, IQR 8 minutes, roughly symmetric middle half, two outliers at 51 and 57 minutes."
- **(B)** "Shop A is faster than Shop B."
- **(C)** "Shop B's delivery times are more variable than Shop A's, because its range (39 minutes) is larger than Shop A's (29 minutes)."
- **(D)** "The median delivery time at Shop A (22 minutes) is shorter than at Shop B (30 minutes). The middle half of Shop A's times is slightly more spread out (IQR 10 minutes against 8), though Shop B's range is larger because of its outliers. Shop A's times are skewed right, while the middle half of Shop B's is roughly symmetric. Shop B has two high outliers, at 51 and 57 minutes; Shop A has none."
- **(E)** "The median of A is lower than the median of B (22 against 30). A's IQR is larger than B's (10 against 8). A is skewed right while B is roughly symmetric. B has two high outliers and A has none."

**14.** Shop A claims: *"Most of our deliveries arrive faster than a typical Shop B delivery."* Which is the best assessment?

- **(A)** Supported. Shop A's Q3 (28 minutes) is below Shop B's median (30 minutes), so at least three quarters of Shop A's deliveries took less time than a typical Shop B delivery.
- **(B)** Not supported, because Shop A's maximum (41 minutes) is almost the same as Shop B's largest non-outlier (42 minutes).
- **(C)** It cannot be assessed without the 80 individual delivery times.
- **(D)** Supported, because Shop A's median is 8 minutes lower than Shop B's.
- **(E)** Not supported, because Shop B's fastest delivery (18 minutes) beat Shop A's median (22 minutes).

**15.** A reader of the boxplots says: *"Shop A's upper whisker is more than twice as long as its lower whisker, so more than twice as many deliveries took 28 to 41 minutes as took 12 to 18 minutes."* The best response is:

- **(A)** Correct. A longer whisker represents more deliveries.
- **(B)** Correct, but only approximately, because whiskers are drawn to the nearest minute.
- **(C)** It cannot be checked without knowing how many deliveries each shop made.
- **(D)** Incorrect. The shorter whisker holds more deliveries, because they are packed more closely together.
- **(E)** Incorrect. Each whisker covers about a quarter of the deliveries. The longer upper whisker means those delivery times are more spread out, not that there are more of them.

**16.** For which shop would you **expect the mean delivery time to be greater than the median**?

- **(A)** Shop A only
- **(B)** Both shops: Shop A because its distribution is skewed right, and Shop B because its two high outliers pull its mean upward
- **(C)** Shop B only
- **(D)** Neither, because boxplots do not show means
- **(E)** Shop A only, because it has the larger IQR

**17.** Shop A's advert says: *"Our electric-bike couriers make us faster than Shop B."* Which is the best assessment?

- **(A)** Supported. Shop A's median is lower, so the electric bikes make it faster.
- **(B)** Not supported, because Shop B has outliers and Shop A does not.
- **(C)** The data support the claim that Shop A's deliveries were generally faster: its median is 8 minutes lower and its Q3 is below Shop B's median. They cannot show that the electric bikes are the **reason**. The student only observed deliveries, and other differences between the shops, such as how far away their customers live, could explain the gap.
- **(D)** Not supported, because the two IQRs are similar.
- **(E)** It cannot be assessed, because the two shops are different sizes.

## Questions 18–20 · z-scores

> Every one of the **1,200 students** in a year group sat both a maths exam and a history exam. For the whole year group, the maths scores had **μ = 64** and **σ = 8**, and the history scores had **μ = 70** and **σ = 5**.

**18.** Priya scored **78 in maths** and **80 in history**. Relative to the year group, in which exam did she do better?

- **(A)** History, because 80 is higher than 78
- **(B)** Maths, because she was 14 marks above the mean, against 10 in history
- **(C)** Equally well in both, because she was above the mean in both
- **(D)** History: her z-score is (80 − 70) ÷ 5 = 2.00, against (78 − 64) ÷ 8 = 1.75 in maths
- **(E)** Maths, because the maths standard deviation is larger

**19.** Another student's maths score has a z-score of **−1.5**. What was the score?

- **(A)** 62.5
- **(B)** 76
- **(C)** 58
- **(D)** It is 1.5 marks below the mean, so it cannot be negative
- **(E)** 52

**20.** At a regional athletics championship, all 300 entrants ran the 100 m and competed in the long jump.

| Event | Mean | Standard deviation | Sam's result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 m (seconds) | 12.0 | 0.5 | 11.2 |
| Long jump (metres) | 5.5 | 0.4 | 6.1 |

Relative to the other entrants, which of Sam's performances was more exceptional?

- **(A)** The 100 m. Its z-score is (11.2 − 12.0) ÷ 0.5 = −1.60, which means 1.6 standard deviations **faster** than the mean, because a lower time is better. The long jump is 1.5 standard deviations better than the mean.
- **(B)** The long jump, because its z-score (1.50) is positive and the 100 m z-score is negative, so the 100 m was below average.
- **(C)** The long jump, because 0.6 m above the mean is more impressive than 0.8 seconds.
- **(D)** Neither can be compared with the other, because seconds and metres are different units.
- **(E)** They were equally exceptional, because both z-scores round to about 1.5.

## Questions 1–6 · The animal shelter

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 1</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

With *n* = 15, the median is the 8th value, **14**. By the course convention it belongs to neither half, so each half has 7 values:

| | Values | Median of the half |
|---|---|---|
| Lower half | 3 5 6 8 9 10 12 | Q1 = **8** |
| Upper half | 15 17 19 22 26 38 58 | Q3 = **22** |

Five-number summary: **3, 8, 14, 22, 58.**

- **(A)** puts the median into both halves, giving 8 values each and Q1 = (8 + 9) ÷ 2 = 8.5, Q3 = (19 + 22) ÷ 2 = 20.5. That is a real method, but not this course's. This is the exact slip the Session 3 script warns about.
- **(C)** puts the **mean** where the median belongs. A five-number summary contains no mean.
- **(D)** replaces the maximum with the upper fence, 43. That confuses the five-number summary, which always uses the true minimum and maximum, with the boxplot's whisker rule. Even the whisker would not end at 43 (see question 3).
- **(E)** thinks outliers are removed from the summary. They are not. The maximum is 58, outlier or not.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 2</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

IQR = 22 − 8 = 14, and 1.5 × 14 = 21.
Lower fence 8 − 21 = −13; upper fence 22 + 21 = **43**.
Only **58** lies beyond a fence. 38 is below 43, so it is not an outlier, however isolated it looks.

- **(A)** uses 1 × IQR instead of 1.5 × IQR: 22 + 14 = 36, which catches 38. Getting the multiplier wrong changes the verdict on the one value that matters.
- **(B)** has not applied the rule, or has compared 58 with the wrong quartile.
- **(C)** flags 3 because it is the smallest value. The lower fence is −13, and no waiting time can be below it.
- **(E)** treats "above Q3" as "outlier". A quarter of any data set lies above Q3 by definition.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 3</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

The whisker extends to the **most extreme data value that is not an outlier** (CED 1.8.A.2). The largest non-outlier is 38.

- **(B)** 43 is the fence, which is a calculated cut-off and not a data value. No dog waited 43 days. This is the "if at 46.25" error from Session 3's commute boxplot.
- **(C)** 58 is the outlier. It gets its own symbol, and the whisker stops short of it.
- **(D)** 22 is Q3, which is the end of the box, not of the whisker.
- **(E)** 36 is the 1 × IQR fence from question 2, option (A).

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 4</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

CED 1.7.A.6: the *p*th percentile is the value with *p*% of the data **less than or equal to it**. Twelve of the 15 waits are 22 days or less, and 12 ÷ 15 = 80%. So 22 days is the **80th percentile**.

- **(A)** is the instructive wrong answer. 22 *is* Q3, and Q3 is *approximately* the 75th percentile. With only 15 values, "approximately" is five percentage points. The CED says "approximately 25%" and "approximately 75%" for exactly this reason. The Session 3 script made the same point with the commute data, where 24% of values were at or below Q1.
- **(B)** uses "less than" instead of "less than or equal to".
- **(D)** gives the position in the ordered list, not a percentage.
- **(E)** confuses the value with its percentile.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 5</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

| Summary | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | 17.47 | **24.13** |
| Standard deviation | 14.45 | **38.14** |
| Range | 55 | **155** |
| Median | 14 | 14 |
| IQR | 14 | 14 |
| Upper fence | 43 | 43 |

58 was the largest value and 158 is still the largest, so every position below it keeps its value. The median, Q1 and Q3, and therefore the IQR and fences, are all **positional** and do not move. The mean, SD and range use the actual values, so all three are dragged by the change. The mean rises by 100 ÷ 15 = 6.67.

- **(A)** treats every statistic as nonresistant.
- **(B)** catches the mean but forgets the SD and range. Both are nonresistant too (CED 1.7.F.1).
- **(C)** thinks the median moves. This was Session 3's teach-it-back question: making the largest value larger does not change which value is in the middle.
- **(D)** has resistance exactly backwards.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 6</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

The waits are skewed right, with a high outlier at 58, and the mean (17.5) sits above the median (14) because the long tail pulls it. Ten of the fifteen dogs waited less than 17.5 days. The median describes a typical dog; the mean describes the tail as much as the middle.

- **(A)** is the answer the Session 3 script warns against for the café: "the mean, because it uses all the data". Using every value is exactly why the mean is pulled by the outlier.
- **(C)** has the vocabulary reversed. The mean is **non**resistant.
- **(D)** is the midrange, which uses only the two most extreme values. It is the least resistant centre possible.
- **(E)** Q3 is a measure of position, not of centre. "Most dogs have been adopted by then" is true of any value above the median.

</details>

## Questions 7–8 · Changing units

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 7</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

Multiplying by 1.8 multiplies the centre **and** the spread. Adding 32 shifts the centre and leaves the spread alone.

- Mean: 1.8 × 20 + 32 = **68 °F**
- SD: 1.8 × 4 = **7.2 °F**
- IQR: 1.8 × 6 = **10.8 °F**

- **(A)** adds 32 to the measures of spread. Spread is a distance between values, and shifting every value by 32 leaves every distance unchanged.
- **(B)** forgets to add 32 to the mean.
- **(D)** changes the mean and nothing else. The multiplication by 1.8 stretches every gap between values, so the spread stretches too (CED 1.7.C.1).
- **(E)** handles the SD correctly and the IQR incorrectly. The IQR is a distance too, so it follows the same rule.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 8</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

Adding the same 2 seconds to every time slides the whole distribution 2 seconds to the right without changing its width. Centres and positions move by 2; spreads do not change.

- **(A)** moves the spreads too. The SD and IQR are distances between values, and adding 2 to both ends of a distance leaves it the same.
- **(B)** mixes the two rules and gets both parts wrong.
- **(C)** confuses the races with the recorded values. The races are the same, but the numbers being summarised are not.
- **(D)** reverses the effect. It is the error of thinking a shift changes the spread and not the centre.

</details>

## Questions 9–12 · Spread and shape from numbers

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 9</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

The SD measures a **typical distance from the mean**, and the range sees only the two ends. In Set II, four of the five values are 4 away from the mean of 10. In Set III, three values sit exactly at the mean.

| Set | Distances from the mean | *s* |
|---|---|---|
| I | 4, 2, 0, 2, 4 | 3.16 |
| **II** | **4, 4, 0, 4, 4** | **4.00** |
| III | 4, 0, 0, 0, 4 | 2.83 |

- **(C)** is the designed trap: same mean and same range, so same spread. The range cannot see anything between the minimum and maximum. That is why the CED lists three measures of variability rather than one.
- **(A)** takes even spacing as a sign of spread. Set I's values are spread evenly, but they are on average closer to the mean than Set II's.
- **(B)** is the set with the **smallest** SD.
- **(E)** is too cautious. The comparison can be made from the distances alone, without finishing the arithmetic.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 10</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

CED 1.7.B.4: the standard deviation is *a typical deviation of the data values from their mean*. The sentence names the reference point, the mean, and it is in context: days and dogs.

- **(B)** is the "apart from what?" error from the Session 3 script. The SD measures distance from the **mean**, not between neighbouring values.
- **(C)** treats the SD as a cut-off for most of the data. That is the empirical rule, which the CED places in topic 2.11 and which does not hold for a skewed distribution anyway.
- **(D)** confuses spread with centre.
- **(E)** measures from the median and treats the SD as half a range. Neither is what an SD is.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 11</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

CED 1.8.B.1: in a left-skewed distribution the mean is usually **smaller** than the median, because the long tail of low values pulls the mean down. Mean 71 with median 78 fits that pattern.

- **(A)** and **(D)** have the mean and median equal or very close, which suggests an approximately symmetric distribution.
- **(B)** and **(E)** have the mean above the median, which suggests skew to the **right**. A student who chose one of these has reversed the relationship. Check it with the tail: the mean follows the tail.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 12</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

A boxplot is a picture of the five-number summary and nothing more. Identical boxplots guarantee the same minimum, Q1, median, Q3 and maximum, so the same median and the same IQR. Everything else is left open.

- **(C)** is the Match Mine lesson from Session 3. Card E was bimodal and card F uniform, and their boxplots were almost identical. CED 1.9.A.1 lists what boxplots can compare: centre, variability, outliers and skewness, but **not** clusters or gaps. Two peaks can sit either side of a median that has almost no data near it.
- **(A)** and **(D)** assume the boxplot fixes the mean and SD. It does not show either, and two data sets with the same five-number summary can have quite different means.
- **(E)** is not fixed either. A boxplot of 20 values and a boxplot of 2,000 can look the same.

</details>

## Questions 13–17 · Two pizza shops

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 13</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

It **compares** on every component, using comparative words (*shorter than*, *more spread out*, *while*, *none*), and it stays in context throughout: delivery times, minutes, shops. It also handles the spread honestly. The IQR and the range point in opposite directions here, and (D) says which is which and why.

- **(A)** is the trap Session 3's Scenario B was built around: **two descriptions are not a comparison.** Every statement in (A) is true, but none of them says how the shops relate. An examiner reading (A) has to do the comparing, and marks are given for doing it.
- **(B)** compares the centres only, and vaguely. "Faster" needs a number and a measure.
- **(C)** compares spread only, and uses the range, which is inflated by Shop B's two outliers. By the resistant measure, the IQR, Shop A's middle half is the more spread out.
- **(E)** is fully comparative and has no context: no delivery times, no minutes. The Session 2 and 3 scripts are explicit that this costs the interpretation mark even when every statistical word is right.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 14</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

This is the positional argument from Session 3's hospital claim. About 75% of Shop A's deliveries took 28 minutes or less. Shop B's median is 30 minutes, so all of those deliveries beat a typical Shop B delivery. "Most" is at least three quarters here, which easily clears half.

- **(D)** reaches the right verdict with weaker evidence. A lower median shows only that **half** of Shop A's deliveries took 22 minutes or less. On its own it does not show that most of them beat 30. The claim is about most deliveries, so the evidence has to reach past half.
- **(B)** and **(E)** compare single extreme values. One fast or slow delivery says nothing about "most".
- **(C)** is too cautious. The quartiles answer this question exactly, because the claim is about positions in each distribution, and positions are what a boxplot shows.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 15</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

Each whisker runs from a quartile to an extreme, so each covers about **25%** of the data (CED 1.8.A.2), whatever its length. Shop A's upper whisker is 13 minutes long and its lower whisker 6, so the slowest quarter of deliveries is more spread out than the fastest quarter. That is right skew, not a larger count.

- **(A)** reads length as quantity. This is the misreading the Session 3 script tells the tutor to prevent before anything else about the boxplot.
- **(B)** accepts the misreading and only questions its precision.
- **(C)** is irrelevant. The quarters are quarters whatever the sample size.
- **(D)** gets the verdict right and invents a reason. The two whiskers hold about the same number of deliveries.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 16</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

CED 1.8.B.1: when a distribution is skewed right, the mean is usually larger than the median. Shop A shows right skew in two places: the median is closer to Q1 than to Q3 (4 minutes against 6), and the upper whisker is much longer than the lower (13 against 6). Shop B's middle half is symmetric, but its two outliers at 51 and 57 minutes are far above everything else, and the mean is not resistant to them.

- **(A)** reads the shape from the box and forgets that outliers also pull the mean.
- **(C)** sees the outliers and misses Shop A's skew.
- **(D)** is right that a boxplot does not show the mean, and wrong to conclude that nothing can be said. Shape and outliers tell you which way the mean is likely to sit.
- **(E)** confuses spread with skew. A larger IQR says nothing about where the mean lies.

## Questions 18–20 · z-scores

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 18</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

z = (*x* − μ) ÷ σ.
Maths: (78 − 64) ÷ 8 = **1.75**. History: (80 − 70) ÷ 5 = **2.00**.
Priya's history score is further above its mean, measured in that exam's own standard deviations, so it is the stronger performance relative to the year group.

- **(A)** reaches the right answer from the raw scores. Had the history mean been 85, 80 would have been below average. Right answer, wrong reason, and on a free-response question the reason is where the mark is.
- **(B)** compares distances above the mean without scaling them. 14 marks in an exam where σ = 8 is less unusual than 10 marks where σ = 5. This is the St Mary's–Riverside error from Session 3.
- **(C)** stops at the sign of the difference. Above the mean in both says nothing about by how much.
- **(E)** turns the reasoning upside down. A larger σ makes the same raw distance **less** unusual.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 19</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

Rearrange z = (*x* − μ) ÷ σ to get *x* = μ + zσ = 64 + (−1.5)(8) = 64 − 12 = **52**.

- **(A)** 62.5 subtracts 1.5 marks rather than 1.5 standard deviations.
- **(B)** 76 adds, ignoring the sign. A negative z-score means below the mean (CED 1.9.D.2).
- **(C)** 58 uses half the correct distance.
- **(D)** misreads what a z-score counts. It counts standard deviations, not marks, and a negative z-score is perfectly ordinary. It means below the mean.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 20</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

100 m: z = (11.2 − 12.0) ÷ 0.5 = **−1.60**. Long jump: z = (6.1 − 5.5) ÷ 0.4 = **1.50**.

The sign says which side of the mean a value is on, not whether it is good. In the 100 m, a time **below** the mean is a faster run, so −1.60 is 1.6 standard deviations better than average. In the long jump, 1.50 is 1.5 standard deviations better. The 100 m is the more exceptional performance.

- **(B)** reads the sign as quality. This is the most instructive error on the test, because it comes from applying the z-score correctly and then interpreting it without context.
- **(C)** compares raw distances in different units. Metres and seconds cannot be compared until each is divided by its own standard deviation. Doing that is what z-scores are for (CED 1.9.E.1).
- **(D)** is the problem z-scores solve. Standardising removes the units, so values from different distributions can be compared.
- **(E)** rounds the difference away. 1.6 against 1.5 is a small difference, but a definite one.

</details>
