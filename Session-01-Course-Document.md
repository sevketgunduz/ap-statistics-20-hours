## Where this session sits

This is the first hour of the course, and it does something the rest of the course depends on: it establishes the **statistical investigation cycle** — pose a question, collect data, analyse data, interpret results — as the frame everything else hangs on. Those four stages are the College Board's four Statistical Practices. Every later session is a tool used at one of these four stages.

Nothing in this session is computational. That is deliberate. Students who cannot say cleanly what a population is, what a sample is, and which numbers are parameters and which are statistics will lose points in Unit 3 and Unit 4 that have nothing to do with their arithmetic.

## Definitions

These are the terms students are accountable for. The wording follows the CED.

### Components of a study

**Statistical study** — a study in which data are collected from a sample to answer an investigative question about a larger population. We run studies because populations are usually too large, or too difficult, to measure completely.

**Datum** — one piece of information about an item or individual. The plural is **data**. A collection of data is a **data set**.

**Population** — all the items or individuals of interest. Its size is written **N**.

**Sample** — a subset of the population from which data are actually obtained. Its size is written **n**.

**In context** — connecting a statistical result back to the real-world situation it came from. Every interpretation on the AP exam must be in context. A number alone never earns the point.

**Investigative question** — the question the study exists to answer. A good one has a defined purpose, can be answered with data you are able to collect, and **is not changed after you see the results**.

### Describing what was measured

**Observational unit** — the item or individual a datum is collected *from*. If you are studying students, one student is one observational unit.

**Variable** — a characteristic that may change from one observational unit to another.

**Parameter** — a numerical summary of a variable for the **population**. Usually unknown.

**Statistic** — a numerical summary of a variable for the **sample**. Known, because you computed it. A statistic is usually not equal to the parameter, but it is the basis for making inferences about it.

> **The memory hook:** **P**opulation goes with **P**arameter. **S**ample goes with **S**tatistic.

### Types of variable

**Categorical variable** (also *qualitative*) — takes values that are category names or group labels.

**Quantitative variable** (also *numerical*) — takes numerical values for a measured or counted quantity, and generally has **units of measure**.

**Discrete quantitative variable** — can take a countable number of values. Typically things you count.

**Continuous quantitative variable** — can take any value within an interval. Between any two possible values there is another possible value. Typically things you measure.

> **The trap:** a variable recorded with digits is not automatically quantitative. Ask whether the numbers have units and whether arithmetic on them means anything. The average of two ZIP codes is meaningless, so ZIP code is categorical.

### Tables for one categorical variable

**Frequency table** — shows the **number** of observational units in each category.

**Relative frequency table** — shows the **proportion** of observational units in each category.

**Proportions, percentages, relative frequencies and ratios** all carry the same information in different clothing. Counts and relative frequencies can be used to justify claims about the variables in context.

## Scenario A — The commute study

> Lincoln High School has **1,842 students**. The principal wants to reorganise the morning bus timetable but does not know how students currently get to school. She asks a randomly chosen **120 students** to record, on one Tuesday: how they primarily travelled to school, how many minutes the journey took, and how many separate vehicles they had to change between.
>
> Of the 120 students surveyed, **43 came by bus**, and their journeys averaged **24.6 minutes**.

### Working through it

**What is the population?** All 1,842 Lincoln High students. **N = 1,842**. Note that the population is defined by the question — the principal is not trying to say anything about students at other schools, so those students are not in the population.

**What is the sample?** The 120 students surveyed. **n = 120**.

**What is the observational unit?** One student. This matters: the data are not about journeys or about buses, they are about students. Every row of the data set is one student.

**What are the variables?** Three were measured on each student:

| Variable | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mode of travel | Categorical | The values are group labels — bus, car, walk, bike |
| Journey time | Quantitative, continuous | Measured in minutes; a journey could take 24.6 minutes, or 24.61 |
| Number of vehicle changes | Quantitative, discrete | Counted; you can change 0, 1 or 2 vehicles, never 1.4 |

**Parameter or statistic?** This is the distinction the whole course rests on.

- The proportion of **all 1,842 students** who come by bus is a **parameter**. The principal does not know it, and this study will not tell her exactly.
- 43/120 = **0.358**, the proportion of the **sampled** students who came by bus, is a **statistic**. She does know it, because she computed it.
- The mean journey time of **all 1,842 students** is a **parameter**. Unknown.
- **24.6 minutes**, the mean for the 120 sampled, is a **statistic**. Known.

The whole of Units 3 and 4 is about the gap between those two columns. For now students only need to name which is which.

**What is the investigative question?** Something like: *What proportion of Lincoln High students travel to school by bus, and how long does the journey take?* It has a defined purpose — redesigning the timetable — and the data needed can actually be collected.

### Where students go wrong

Suppose the principal looks at the results, notices that cyclists have unusually long journeys, and rewrites her report to answer *"Why do cyclists take so long?"*

That breaks **1.1.B.1**: an investigative question should not be changed based on the data analysis or results. The cyclist pattern might be real, or it might be four unusual students out of 120. It is a legitimate question for a **new** study with **new** data — it is not something this study can answer, because the question was chosen *because* of the pattern.

## Scenario B — The hospital discharge survey

> A regional hospital network discharged **12,400 patients** last year across six hospitals. To review its care, the network surveys **300** of those patients. For each one it records: which hospital treated them, their room number, the number of nights they stayed, and the time in minutes from arrival to being seen by a doctor.
>
> The results by hospital: **St Mary's 84, Riverside 71, Northgate 52, Parkview 45, Eastwood 30, Hillcrest 18.**

### Working through it

**Population:** all 12,400 patients discharged last year, **N = 12,400**.
**Sample:** the 300 surveyed, **n = 300**.
**Observational unit:** one discharged patient.

**The variables — and the trap:**

| Variable | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital | Categorical | Six group labels |
| Room number | **Categorical** | Written as digits, but it is a label. Room 305 is not "more" than room 204, and the average room number means nothing. **No units of measure.** |
| Nights stayed | Quantitative, discrete | A count. You stay 3 nights or 4, never 3.5 |
| Minutes until seen by a doctor | Quantitative, continuous | Measured on a scale; 47.3 minutes is possible |

Room number is the point of this scenario. Whenever a variable is written with digits, the test is: **does it have units, and does arithmetic on it mean anything?** Nights has units (nights) and averaging is meaningful. Room number has neither.

### Building the tables (Topic 1.3)

**Frequency table** — counts in each category:

| Hospital | Frequency |
|---|---|
| St Mary's | 84 |
| Riverside | 71 |
| Northgate | 52 |
| Parkview | 45 |
| Eastwood | 30 |
| Hillcrest | 18 |
| **Total** | **300** |

**Relative frequency table** — proportions of the sample:

| Hospital | Relative frequency | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| St Mary's | 0.280 | 28.0% |
| Riverside | 0.237 | 23.7% |
| Northgate | 0.173 | 17.3% |
| Parkview | 0.150 | 15.0% |
| Eastwood | 0.100 | 10.0% |
| Hillcrest | 0.060 | 6.0% |
| **Total** | **1.000** | **100%** |

Each relative frequency is the count divided by 300. The column must total 1 (or 100%), allowing for rounding — a useful self-check for students.

### Justifying a claim in context (1.3.B.2)

A hospital administrator claims: *"Most of our discharged patients came from St Mary's."*

The table does not support it. St Mary's accounts for 28.0% of the sample — the largest single share, but nowhere near a majority. A defensible version of the claim would be: *"St Mary's accounted for more discharged patients than any other single hospital in the sample, at 28.0%, though it still treated fewer than three in ten."*

This is the shape of every justification in this course: **state the claim, quote the number, say what the number does and does not establish, keep it in context.**

## Classroom activity — Population or Sample?

**Format:** groups of four · 10 minutes · one card set per group

**Materials:** eight short study descriptions on cards. Six should be unambiguous; two should be genuinely arguable.

**Task:** for each card the group identifies the population, the sample, the observational unit, one variable, and that variable's type.

**How to run it:** give groups six minutes on all eight, then spend four minutes on the two ambiguous cards only. The argument is the lesson — students discover that the population is not a property of the data, it is a property of **the question being asked**. Two researchers with the same data set can have different populations if they are asking different questions.

**Suggested ambiguous cards:**

1. *A researcher tests every lightbulb coming off one production line during one shift, to judge the quality of the factory's output.* — Is this a census of that shift, or a sample of the factory's ongoing production? It depends on the investigative question.
2. *A teacher records the marks of all 28 students in her class to decide whether to re-teach a topic to that class.* — Here there is no sampling at all. The population is the 28 students and she has measured all of them, so her figures are parameters, not statistics.

Card 2 is the more valuable of the two: it shows that "the mean" is a parameter or a statistic depending entirely on whether you measured everybody.

3. Give one example of a discrete quantitative variable and one continuous quantitative variable that could both be measured on the same observational unit.

### Exit ticket

1. A parameter summarises the **population** and is usually unknown; a statistic summarises the **sample** and is known because it was computed from collected data.
2. **Categorical.** The numbers are labels identifying players, not measured quantities — they have no units and arithmetic on them is meaningless.
3. Accept any valid pair on one unit, e.g. for one student: number of siblings (discrete), height in cm (continuous).

### Part B (12 marks)

**B1.** All 34,000 registered library members; N = 34,000. (1)
**B2.** The 450 members contacted; n = 450. (1)
**B3.** One registered library member. (1)
**B4.** (6 — award per correct cell)

| Variable | Type | Discrete/continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Branch used most often | Categorical | — |
| Number of items borrowed | Quantitative | Discrete |
| Ever used e-book service | Categorical | — |
| Hours spent in buildings | Quantitative | Continuous |

**B5.** A **statistic** (1) — it is computed from the 450 members in the sample, not from all 34,000 members of the population. (1)
**B6.** Any population quantity, e.g. "the true proportion of all 34,000 registered members who have used the e-book service", or "the mean number of items borrowed by all 34,000 members". (1)

*Common error to watch for in B5:* students who answer "parameter" because the number concerns library members generally. Push them back to the arithmetic — 450 is the denominator, so the sample is what was measured.

### Part C (6 marks)

**C1.** Not a good question. (1) "Any good" is not defined and cannot be measured — no data collection would settle it. Rewrite must name a measurable variable, e.g. *"What proportion of registered members visited a branch at least once in the last year?"* (1)

**C2.** Good question. (1) It has a defined purpose, names a specific measurable variable, and the required data can be collected from members. (1)

**C3.** Not acceptable **for this study**. (1) The question was chosen after seeing the results, which violates the principle that an investigative question should not be changed based on the data analysis. The Eastside pattern may be real or may be chance variation in this sample. It is a legitimate question for a **new** study collecting **new** data. (1)

*C3 is the question that separates students who have understood 1.1.B from those who have memorised it. Accept any answer that identifies the after-the-fact selection as the fault.*

**D1.** (3)

| Mode | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Bus | 17 |
| Car | 10 |
| Walk | 8 |
| Bike | 3 |
| Train | 2 |
| **Total** | **40** |

| Mode | Relative frequency | Percentage |
|---|---|---|

**D3.** 0.425 + 0.250 + 0.200 + 0.075 + 0.050 = 1.000 ✓ (1)

**D4.** (2) The claim is **not** supported. Bus is the most common mode at 42.5%, but "most" means more than half, and 42.5% is below 50%. A supportable version: *"More students travel by bus than by any other single mode, at 42.5%, but fewer than half the class travels by bus."*

### Part E (4 marks)

**E1.** (2) Number of nights is quantitative because it is a counted quantity with a unit of measure (nights), and differences between values are meaningful — 5 nights is two more than 3 nights. Room number has no unit of measure; it is a label identifying a location, so it is categorical even though it is written with digits.

**E2.** (2) The mean number of nights is meaningful — it is the average length of stay and could inform staffing. The mean room number is meaningless, because room numbers are labels; averaging them produces a number that corresponds to nothing, and may not even be a real room.
