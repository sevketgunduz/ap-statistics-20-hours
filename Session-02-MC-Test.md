## Before you start

Every question is set in a scenario, and none of the scenarios is one you worked in Session 2. That is deliberate: recognising the hospital survey or the commute times again would test your memory, not your statistics. The exam hands you a display you have never seen and asks what it shows. So does this test.

Three kinds of question appear. Some ask you to **read or build** a display. Some ask you to **judge a claim** someone has made from a display. The hardest ask you to **spot what a display cannot do**: when a pie chart is the wrong choice, when a histogram hides something, when a single centre describes nobody.

You will need a pen and paper for working. Two questions are about graphs, which are printed with them.

# The test

## Questions 1–4 · The gym

> A gym recorded the type of class booked for every one of its **400 class bookings** in one month.

| Class | Bookings |
|---|---|
| Spin | 120 |
| Yoga | 96 |
| HIIT | 80 |
| Pilates | 64 |
| Boxing | 40 |
| **Total** | **400** |

**1.** A pie chart of these bookings is drawn. The angle of the Yoga slice is

- **(A)** 96.0°
- **(B)** 24.0°
- **(C)** 86.4°
- **(D)** 43.2°
- **(E)** 72.0°

**2.** A second gym publishes a pie chart of its **1,200** class bookings. Its Boxing slice has an angle of **54°**. How many of that gym's bookings were for Boxing?

- **(A)** 15
- **(B)** 54
- **(C)** 648
- **(D)** 180
- **(E)** It cannot be determined without the angles of the other slices

**3.** The first gym's manager claims: *"Spin and Yoga together make up more than half of all our bookings."* Which is the best assessment?

- **(A)** Supported: Spin is 30.0% and Yoga 24.0% of bookings, so together they are 54.0%, which is more than half.
- **(B)** Not supported: the average of the two shares is (30.0% + 24.0%) ÷ 2 = 27.0%, well under half.
- **(C)** Not supported, because no single class accounts for more than half of the bookings.
- **(D)** Supported, because Spin is the most frequently booked class.
- **(E)** It cannot be assessed from a table; a bar chart would be needed.

**4.** The gym also asked **200 members**: *"Which facilities have you used this month? Tick all that apply."* The results were Weights room 124, Pool 90, Studio classes 70, Sauna 46 — a total of 330 ticks. A trainee plans to show these results as a pie chart. Which is the best advice?

- **(A)** A pie chart is fine, with each slice sized by its share of the 330 ticks.
- **(B)** A pie chart is fine, with each slice sized by the percentage of the 200 members who ticked it.
- **(C)** The results should be shown as a histogram, because the counts are quantitative.
- **(D)** No graph can be drawn, because members could tick more than one box.
- **(E)** A pie chart is not appropriate. Members could tick several boxes, so the percentages of members total 165%, not 100%, and the slices would not be parts of one whole. A bar chart of the percentage of members using each facility is appropriate.

## Questions 5–7 · Two towns

> Two towns each surveyed working residents about how they usually travel to work. **Town A surveyed 500 people. Town B surveyed 200.**

| Mode | Town A | Town B |
|---|---|---|
| Car | 260 | 90 |
| Bus | 120 | 40 |
| Bike | 70 | 44 |
| Walk | 50 | 26 |
| **Total** | **500** | **200** |

**5.** A councillor says: *"Cycling to work is more common in Town A than in Town B — 70 cyclists against 44."* Which is the best assessment?

- **(A)** Supported, because 70 is greater than 44.
- **(B)** Not supported. The towns surveyed different numbers of people. As shares, 14.0% of Town A's respondents cycle against 22.0% of Town B's, so cycling is more common in Town B.
- **(C)** Not supported, because Town B has far fewer car users than Town A.
- **(D)** Supported, because a higher percentage of Town A's respondents cycle.
- **(E)** It cannot be assessed, because the two towns surveyed different numbers of people.

**6.** Which display gives a **valid** visual comparison of how people travel to work in the two towns?

- **(A)** A side-by-side bar chart of the counts in the table
- **(B)** A single pie chart of all 700 respondents combined
- **(C)** Side-by-side bar charts of the relative frequencies, or a separate pie chart for each town
- **(D)** A histogram of mode of transport for each town
- **(E)** A dotplot of the 700 responses

**7.** For which mode of transport does the **share** of respondents differ most between the two towns?

- **(A)** Car
- **(B)** Bus
- **(C)** Walk
- **(D)** Bike
- **(E)** The shares cannot be compared, because the towns surveyed different numbers of people

## Questions 11–13 · Phone batteries

> A consumer magazine tested **40 phones** of one model, recording how long each battery lasted, in hours, under a standard test. Each bin includes its left edge and excludes its right: the first bar is 8 to under 9 hours.

![Histogram of battery life in hours for 40 phones](figures/t02-hist-battery.svg)

*Bin width 1 hour. The number above each bar is its frequency.*

**11.** What percentage of the phones had a battery life of **under 11 hours**?

- **(A)** 32.5%
- **(B)** 17.5%
- **(C)** 15.0%
- **(D)** 67.5%
- **(E)** 13%

**12.** Which of the following can **not** be determined from the histogram alone?

- **(A)** The number of phones whose battery lasted under 10 hours
- **(B)** The exact battery life of the longest-lasting phone
- **(C)** The interval containing the most phones
- **(D)** The number of phones tested
- **(E)** The general shape of the distribution

**13.** The manufacturer advertises: *"Most of our phones last at least 13 hours."* Which is the best assessment?

- **(A)** Supported, because the distribution extends beyond 13 hours.
- **(B)** Supported, because the 13 to 14 hour bar is one of the taller bars on the right-hand side.
- **(C)** It cannot be assessed, because the histogram does not show individual battery lives.
- **(D)** Not supported: only 5 + 3 = 8 of the 40 phones, or 20%, lasted at least 13 hours. Most phones lasted between 10 and 13 hours.
- **(E)** Not supported, because only 3 phones lasted at least 14 hours.

## Questions 14–16 · The family science workshop

> A museum ran a Saturday science workshop for families. The ages of all **22 people** who attended are shown in the stem-and-leaf plot.

```
Key: 3 | 4 = 34 years

0 | 6 7 7 8 8 9 9
1 | 0 0 1 1 2
2 |
3 | 4 5 6 8 9
4 | 0 1 2 3 5
```

**14.** Which best describes the distribution of ages?

- **(A)** Skewed right, because the first row of the stemplot is the longest.
- **(B)** Unimodal and approximately symmetric.
- **(C)** Bimodal, with two clusters — ages 6 to 12 and 34 to 45 — separated by a gap in which no attendee was aged 13 to 33.
- **(D)** Approximately uniform, because three of the rows have five leaves each.
- **(E)** Unimodal, with every attendee aged over 30 an outlier.

**15.** The ages are redrawn as a histogram with bins of width **25**, starting at 0: that is, 0 to under 25 and 25 to under 50. Which statement about this histogram is correct?

- **(A)** It has two bars, of heights 12 and 10. The gap and the two separate clusters are no longer visible.
- **(B)** It looks the same as a histogram with bins of width 10, because changing the bin width does not change the appearance of a histogram.
- **(C)** It has two bars, of heights 12 and 10, which shows that the distribution is skewed right.
- **(D)** It has five bars, of heights 7, 5, 0, 5 and 5.
- **(E)** It cannot be drawn, because a histogram needs at least five bins.

**16.** The museum's leaflet says: *"The typical attendee at our family workshop was about 23 years old."* (The mean age of the 22 attendees is 22.8 years.) Which is the best critique?

- **(A)** The claim is reasonable, because 23 lies between the youngest attendee, 6, and the oldest, 45.
- **(B)** The typical age should have been given as 25.5, halfway between 6 and 45.
- **(C)** The claim is reasonable, because an average always describes a typical member of the group.
- **(D)** The leaflet should have said 22, because ages are recorded in whole years.
- **(E)** No attendee was aged between 13 and 33. The ages form two clusters, children aged 6 to 12 and adults aged 34 to 45, so no single age describes a typical attendee. A better summary describes the two groups separately.

- **(B)** 36 cm
- **(C)** 34 cm

**19.** Eighty people were asked for their favourite ice-cream flavour. A student draws a bar chart with the bars in the order Vanilla, Chocolate, Strawberry, Mint, Other. - **(A)** Skewness describes the distribution of a quantitative variable, whose values have a natural order. Flavours can be listed in any order, and reordering the bars would change the apparent "shape", so calling this distribution skewed has no meaning.
- **(B)** The student has the direction wrong. The tallest bar is on the left, so the distribution is skewed left.
- **(C)** The description is correct, because the bars trail off toward the right.
- **(D)** The description is correct, but the bars must touch for the shape to be read.
- **(E)** The student should have drawn a histogram of the flavours, which would show the shape more clearly.

The shape of the distribution of the outcomes is best described as

- **(A)** unimodal, with its peak at 2
- **(B)** skewed left
- **(C)** approximately uniform — every face occurred close to 100 times, with no prominent peak
- **(D)** bimodal, with peaks at 2 and 5

## Answers 1–4 · The gym

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 1</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

Yoga's relative frequency is 96 ÷ 400 = 0.240, so its slice angle is 0.240 × 360° = **86.4°**.

| Class | *f* | *rf* | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spin | 120 | 0.300 | 108.0° |
| Yoga | 96 | 0.240 | 86.4° |
| HIIT | 80 | 0.200 | 72.0° |
| Pilates | 64 | 0.160 | 57.6° |
| Boxing | 40 | 0.100 | 36.0° |
| **Total** | **400** | **1.000** | **360.0°** |

- **(A)** 96.0° uses the **count as the angle**. It only looks plausible because 96 happens to be a sensible-sized angle. Had there been 400 Yoga bookings, it would have given 400°.
- **(B)** 24.0° uses the **percentage as the angle**. A percentage is out of 100; a circle is out of 360.
- **(D)** 43.2° is half the correct angle. It comes from mixing up 180° and 360°.
- **(E)** 72.0° is HIIT's angle: a wrong-row error.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 2</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

The same formula run backwards. The slice is 54 ÷ 360 = 0.15 of the circle, so Boxing is 0.15 of the bookings: 0.15 × 1,200 = **180**.

- **(A)** 15 is the **percentage**, not the count. The calculation stopped one step early.
- **(B)** 54 reads the **angle as the count**, the reverse of question 1's option (A).
- **(C)** 648 treats 54 as a **percentage** (54% of 1,200). Angles are out of 360, not 100.
- **(E)** is the instructive wrong answer. Each slice's angle, **on its own**, is its share of the whole. That is exactly what a pie chart encodes, so the other slices are not needed.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 3</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

Spin 120 ÷ 400 = 30.0%, Yoga 96 ÷ 400 = 24.0%. Both shares are of the **same total**, so they add: 54.0%, which is more than half. You could also add the counts: 216 of 400.

- **(B)** **averages** the two percentages. This is the adding-versus-averaging error from the Session 1 test, question 9. It comes back because it feels like "combining" two numbers. Shares of one total are added.
- **(C)** answers a different claim. No one said a single class was a majority.
- **(D)** reaches "supported" for a reason that has nothing to do with the claim. Spin being the largest does not show that two classes together pass 50%.
- **(E)** is false. The table has everything needed. A graph shows the same information in a different form; it adds none.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 4</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

A pie chart shows **parts of one whole**. CED 1.4.A.2 says the areas of the slices together equal 1, or 100% of the total area. That works only if each individual falls into exactly one category. Here a member can tick several boxes:

| Facility | Members | % of 200 members |
|---|---|---|
| Weights room | 124 | 62.0% |
| Pool | 90 | 45.0% |
| Studio classes | 70 | 35.0% |
| Sauna | 46 | 23.0% |
| **Total** | **330 ticks** | **165.0%** |

No circle can hold 165%. A bar chart of the percentage of members using each facility is honest: each bar stands alone, and nothing requires the bars to sum to 100%.

- **(A)** forces the data into a pie by using the 330 ticks as the whole. The chart can then be drawn, but its slices mean nothing a reader would want. Weights would show as 124 ÷ 330 = 37.6%, when in fact **62.0% of members** used the weights room. This is the most dangerous option because it produces a chart that looks correct.
- **(B)** cannot be drawn. The slices would total 165% of a circle.
- **(C)** confuses the counts in a table with the variable being displayed. The variable is *facility used*, which is categorical. Every table of a categorical variable contains counts.
- **(D)** overcorrects. A bar chart handles tick-all-that-apply data well.

</details>

## Answers 5–7 · Two towns

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 5</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

| Mode | Town A *f* | Town A % | Town B *f* | Town B % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Car | 260 | 52.0% | 90 | 45.0% |
| Bus | 120 | 24.0% | 40 | 20.0% |
| Bike | 70 | 14.0% | 44 | 22.0% |
| Walk | 50 | 10.0% | 26 | 13.0% |
| **Total** | **500** | **100%** | **200** | **100%** |

Town A surveyed two and a half times as many people, so it has more of **everything**. Its larger cyclist count is mostly a larger survey. As shares, cycling is clearly more common in Town B.

- **(A)** compares raw counts across data sets with different totals. This is the comparison trap from Session 2, Scenario A, and it is the single most examined idea in topic 1.4.
- **(C)** gives a true fact that has no bearing on cycling.
- **(D)** is factually false. Always compute the percentage before quoting it.
- **(E)** is the overcorrection. Different totals do not prevent a comparison; they only prevent a comparison of **counts**. Converting to relative frequencies is exactly what makes the comparison possible.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 6</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

CED 1.4.C.1: relative frequency tables, bar charts and pie charts can all be used to compare data sets on the same categorical variable. With unequal totals, the display must show **relative** frequencies. One pie per town does this automatically, because each pie is its own whole.

- **(A)** is the graphical version of question 5's trap. Town A's bars would be taller in every category, so the chart would show survey size, not travel habits.
- **(B)** merges the two towns into one whole, which destroys the comparison it was supposed to make.
- **(D)** and **(E)** are displays for **quantitative** variables. Mode of transport is categorical: its values are labels, with no numerical scale to put on an axis.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 7</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

| Mode | Town A | Town B | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Car | 52.0% | 45.0% | 7.0 pp |
| Bus | 24.0% | 20.0% | 4.0 pp |
| **Bike** | **14.0%** | **22.0%** | **8.0 pp** |
| Walk | 10.0% | 13.0% | 3.0 pp |

Bike differs by 8.0 percentage points, just ahead of Car at 7.0.

- **(A)** Car is the designed trap. It has by far the biggest **count** difference (260 against 90, a gap of 170), and it is the largest category in both towns. Neither fact is the question asked. Car is also a close second on shares, so a student who picks it after computing shares has made a subtraction slip. A student who picks it without computing has fallen for the counts.
- **(B)** and **(C)** have the smallest differences.
- **(E)** is the same overcorrection as question 5, option (E).

</details>

## Answers 8–10 · The quiz

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 8</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

The bulk of the scores sits at 8, 9 and 10. The tail stretches **left**, through 7, 6 and 5, down to the single 2. Skew is named for the tail, so the distribution is **skewed left** (negatively skewed).

- **(A)** is the error the Session 2 tutor script calls the most common in Unit 1. It names the skew after where the bulk sits. The reasoning in (A), "most of the scores are on the right", is true. It is the reason for the **opposite** answer.
- **(C)** confuses symmetry with range. Scores covering the scale says nothing about whether one half mirrors the other.
- **(D)** mistakes one isolated dot for a second group. A single point far from the rest is a possible outlier, not a second mode. Bimodal needs two **prominent** peaks (CED 1.6.A.3).
- **(E)** confuses "every value occurred" with "every value occurred about equally often". The stacks run from 1 to 9 dots, which is nowhere near uniform.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 9</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

At least 8 means 8, 9 or 10: 7 + 9 + 6 = 22 students, and 22 ÷ 30 = **0.733**.

- **(A)** 0.233 counts only the students who scored exactly 8 (7 ÷ 30). "At least" was read as "exactly".
- **(B)** 0.500 counts 9 and 10 only (15 ÷ 30). It misses the boundary value: "at least 8" **includes** 8.
- **(D)** 0.867 counts from 7 upward (26 ÷ 30), one stack too many.
- **(E)** 0.267 is the complement (8 ÷ 30): the students who scored **below** 8.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 10</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

Nine students scored 9 and six scored 10, so 15 of the 30 scored 9 or 10. That is **exactly half**, not more than half. Statement V would be true as *"half of the students scored 9 or 10"*.

- **(A)** I is correct. The tail runs left (see question 8).
- **(B)** II is correct. The score of 2 is three points below the next-lowest score. CED 1.6.A.4 defines an outlier as a value unusually small or large relative to the rest, and that describes it. The formal 1.5 × IQR test comes in Session 3.
- **(C)** III is correct. A gap is a region with no observed data (CED 1.6.A.5), and nobody scored 3 or 4.
- **(D)** IV is correct. There is one main peak, at 9.

This question is about checking every claim against the display, not just the claims that sound statistical. The error is a counting mistake inside an otherwise excellent description. Free-response graders read every sentence, so one false number costs the mark even when the vocabulary is perfect.

</details>

## Answers 11–13 · Phone batteries

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 11</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

Under 11 hours means the first three bins: 2 + 4 + 7 = 13 phones, and 13 ÷ 40 = **32.5%**.

- **(B)** 17.5% is the 10 to 11 bin alone (7 ÷ 40). It reads the bar just below 11 as though it were the answer.
- **(C)** 15.0% stops one bin early (6 ÷ 40, under 10 hours).
- **(D)** 67.5% is the complement: phones lasting 11 hours or more.
- **(E)** 13% is the **count** with a percent sign added. The division by 40 is missing.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 12</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

A histogram puts values into bins (CED 1.5.A.2) and throws away the individual values to do it. All we know is that the longest-lasting phones are somewhere from 14 to under 15 hours. The longest might have lasted 14.1 hours or 14.9.

- **(A)**, **(C)** and **(D)** can all be read directly. Under 10 hours is 2 + 4 = 6 phones, the tallest bar is 11 to 12 hours, and the frequencies total 40.
- **(E)** is what a histogram does **best**. It gives up individual values precisely so that the shape is easier to see. This trade-off is the one from Session 2's Build & Defend activity.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 13</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

The claim is about a **majority**. The bins that make up "at least 13 hours" are 13 to 14 and 14 to 15, holding 5 + 3 = 8 phones, which is 8 ÷ 40 = 20%. That is nowhere near most. A supportable version would be: *"Most of our phones last between 10 and 13 hours, and one in five lasts 13 hours or longer."*

- **(A)** confuses "some values reach this far" with "most values are here". The distribution extending past 13 hours is what makes the claim possible, not what makes it true.
- **(B)** judges the bar by its position on the page, not by what it counts.
- **(C)** is wrong **here**, though the instinct is a good one. A histogram cannot answer every question about thresholds. This one it can, because 13 hours falls exactly on a bin edge, so the phones at or above 13 hours are exactly the phones in the last two bars. A threshold such as 13.5 hours, in the middle of a bin, would make (C) correct.
- **(E)** reaches the right verdict by the wrong count. It forgot that the 13 to 14 bin is included in "at least 13".

</details>

## Answers 14–16 · The family science workshop

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 14</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

The two peaks make it **bimodal** (CED 1.6.A.3). The two concentrations are **clusters** (1.6.A.6), and the empty `2 |` row, together with nobody aged 13 to 19 or 30 to 33, is a **gap** (1.6.A.5). The context explains it: this is a family workshop, attended by children and their parents.

- **(A)** reads the length of the first row as the whole story and ignores the empty row that splits the data in two.
- **(B)** has not seen the gap. It is what happens when a stemplot is read row by row instead of as a picture.
- **(D)** confuses equal row lengths with equal frequencies across the whole range. A uniform distribution has no gaps and no prominent peaks, and this one has an empty row in the middle.
- **(E)** labels half the data set as outliers. An outlier is unusual **relative to the rest of the data**, and ten of twenty-two people cannot be unusual relative to the other twelve. They are a cluster.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 15</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

Ages 6 to 12 all fall in 0 to under 25, giving 12. Ages 34 to 45 all fall in 25 to under 50, giving 10. Two adjacent bars with nothing between them: the gap, the two clusters and the bimodal shape have all disappeared. This is CED 1.5.A.2, *altering the bin widths can change the appearance of the histogram*, at its most extreme. The data are the same; the picture tells a different story.

- **(B)** contradicts that essential-knowledge statement directly.
- **(C)** gets the bars right and then reads a shape into two bars, which is too few bars to show any shape. The picture has lost the information; it has not revealed new information.
- **(D)** gives the stem counts, which is the bin-width-10 histogram.
- **(E)** invents a rule. Bin choices can be good or bad, but there is no minimum number of bins.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 16</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

The arithmetic is not the problem: the mean of the 22 ages really is 22.8. The problem is that the number describes **no one**. There were no 23-year-olds at the workshop, and nobody within ten years of 23. When a distribution has two clusters, a single "typical" value falls in the gap between them. CED 1.6.B.1 says a graph can reveal information that justifies, or here undermines, a claim. The stemplot shows immediately what the single number hides.

- **(A)** treats "within the range" as "typical". Every average lies within the range; that does not make it typical.
- **(B)** swaps one misleading single number for another. The midrange, 25.5, lies in the same gap.
- **(C)** states the belief the question was designed to break. An average describes the centre of a distribution, and a centre is typical only when the distribution has one main peak.
- **(D)** is the rounding error from the Session 1 test, question 14. A mean does not have to be a value the variable can take.

Session 3 makes this precise with the mean and median. The lesson here comes first, and it matters more: **look at the shape before you trust any centre.**

</details>

## Answers 17–22 · Separate questions

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 17</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

Plot III is the only one that satisfies both CED 1.5.A.3 rules and keeps the empty stem.

- **Plot I** omits the empty `4 |` row. That row is the gap between 38 and 52 minutes. Without it, 52 and 57 sit directly under the 30s, and the plot's shape suggests a continuous run of values that does not exist. This is the Session 2 rule: *do not let them omit the empty row*.
- **Plot II** keeps the empty row but leaves the leaves in the order the data were listed. The CED requires both stems **and leaves** to be ordered smallest to largest. An unordered stemplot still shows the shape, but it cannot be used to read off the smallest, largest or middle value of a row.
- **(C)** and **(E)** accept Plot I. This is the commonest wrong answer, because Plot I looks tidy.
- **(D)** accepts Plot II and misses the ordering rule.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 18</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**

bin width = (maximum − minimum) ÷ number of bins = (204 − 168) ÷ 6 = 36 ÷ 6 = **6 cm**

- **(A)** 4 divides the **number of players** by the number of bins (24 ÷ 6). The number of observations plays no part in bin width.
- **(B)** 36 is the range, not divided by anything.
- **(C)** 34 divides the maximum alone by 6. The minimum must be subtracted first.
- **(E)** 28 divides the minimum alone by 6.

> **A practical note for the tutor.** With "left edge in, right edge out" bins starting at 168, a width of exactly 6 puts the 204 cm player on the right-hand edge of the last bin, 198 to under 204. In practice the last bin is closed on the right, or the width is rounded up slightly. The formula gives the width. It does not decide the edges, and questions that ask for the width expect the formula.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 19</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**

Skewed, symmetric, tail: every shape word in CED 1.6.A.2 is defined by reference to **smaller and larger values**, and flavours have no smaller or larger. Put Mint first and the "skew" changes. Sort alphabetically and it changes again. A shape that depends on an arbitrary ordering is not a property of the data.

- **(B)** accepts the premise and argues only about direction. It is the tail/bulk confusion from question 8, applied to a display where the question should not arise at all.
- **(C)** accepts the description outright.
- **(D)** is backwards. Bars in a bar chart should **not** touch, because the categories are separate. Bars in a histogram touch because the scale is continuous.
- **(E)** proposes a quantitative display for a categorical variable, the same confusion as question 6, options (D) and (E).

A bar chart of categorical data can still be described: which category is most common, which categories are rare, how the shares compare. It cannot be described as skewed.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 20</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**

CED 1.6.A.3: a distribution in which each frequency is **approximately** the same, with no prominent peaks, is approximately uniform. The frequencies run from 95 to 104 against an expected 100 each. A fair die rolled 600 times would never give exactly 100 of every face, because that is not how chance behaves.

- **(A)** and **(D)** read ordinary chance variation as structure. A difference of 104 against 100 is not a **prominent** peak. It is the variation that Session 1's Sample & Estimate tool showed a statistic always has.
- **(B)** attaches a skew to frequencies that are flat.
- **(E)** is wrong about the variable. The face showing is a **count of spots**, quantitative and discrete, with a natural order from 1 to 6. This is the opposite situation to question 19: here the order is real, so shape words apply.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 21</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**

Eighteen values is a comfortable size for a stemplot. It shows the shape **and** keeps every individual value, so the fastest time can be read off the first leaf. A histogram would show the shape but lose the exact value.

- **(A)** is a job for a histogram. With 3,400 values, a stemplot would have hundreds of leaves on each row.
- **(E)** is the trap. Keeping every individual value is a stemplot's strength, but with 3,400 values no display makes each one "easily read". Dotplots and stemplots become unreadable when *n* is large, as the Session 2 reference sheet puts it.
- **(C)** and **(D)** involve categorical variables, which call for bar charts or pie charts. Neither a stemplot nor a histogram applies.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 22</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**

It covers all four components the CED lists in 1.6.A.1, **in context**:

| Component | The words in (E) |
|---|---|
| Shape | roughly symmetric, unimodal, peak between 11 and 12 hours |
| Centre | about 11 to 12 hours |
| Variability | from between 8 and 9 hours up to between 14 and 15 hours |
| Unusual features | no apparent outliers or gaps |
| **Context** | battery life, 40 phones, hours |

It also states the range honestly as bin intervals, because the histogram does not show the exact extremes (question 12).

- **(A)** gives shape only. This is the commonest lost mark in Unit 1: describe the shape and stop.
- **(B)** has every statistical component and no context. There is no variable, no units and no phones: "a centre of about 11 to 12" of what? The Session 2 tutor script is explicit that this loses the interpretation mark even when every statistical word is right. It also quotes 8 and 15 as if they were the exact extremes.
- **(C)** names the skew incorrectly and for a wrong reason. The distribution is close to symmetric, and in any case the tallest bar's position does not determine skew; the tails do.
- **(D)** is a claim, not a description, and it is false. Only 11 phones fall in the 11 to 12 hour bin, and none can be said to last exactly 11 hours.

</details>
