Session 6 — Multiple-Choice Test

AP Statistics · Twenty-Hour Course · CED Topics 1.10, 1.11 20 questions · 35 minutes · no calculator needed

Before you start

None of the scenarios here is one you worked in Session 6. The Lincoln High sampling plans, the St Mary's reminders, the café's loyalty members, the hospital network and Oakfield's roll have all been left out. Every question hands you a new study and asks you to classify it, criticise it, or say how far its conclusion can reach.

The test uses the session's conventions throughout:

  • Sampling methods are named in full: simple random, stratified random, cluster random or systematic random sample. "Random sample" alone never identifies a method.
  • Stratified takes some individuals from every group; cluster takes every individual from some groups.
  • A simple random sample of size n is one where every possible sample of size n is equally likely. Giving every individual the same chance is not enough on its own.
  • The test of an experiment is whether the researcher imposed the treatments. Random selection licenses generalising to the population; random assignment licenses conclusions about cause.

The vocabulary of bias (undercoverage, voluntary response and so on) belongs to Session 7, and no question needs it.

The test

Questions 1–5 · The commuter train

A rail company wants to know how satisfied its passengers are with the 07:40 commuter train. The train has 12 carriages:
- 2 first-class carriages, each carrying 120 passengers
- 10 standard carriages, each carrying 72 passengers
That is 960 passengers in all: 240 in first class and 720 in standard. The company can afford to survey 80.

1. Plan 1: "Number the 12 carriages, use a random number generator to choose 3 of them, and survey every passenger in those 3 carriages." This is a

  • (A) stratified random sample, because the train is divided into carriages
  • (B) simple random sample, because the carriages were chosen at random
  • (C) cluster random sample
  • (D) systematic random sample, because carriages are numbered in order
  • (E) census of the 3 carriages, and so a census of the train

2. Plan 2: "Choose 20 first-class passengers at random from the 240, and 60 standard passengers at random from the 720." This is a

  • (A) stratified random sample
  • (B) cluster random sample, because the passengers are grouped by class
  • (C) simple random sample, because every passenger has the same chance, 1 in 12
  • (D) systematic random sample
  • (E) convenience sample, because the classes are fixed in advance

3. Plan 3: "As passengers pass the ticket barrier, choose a random number from 1 to 12. Survey that passenger and every 12th passenger after them." This is a

  • (A) simple random sample, because the starting point is random
  • (B) stratified random sample, with 12 strata
  • (C) cluster random sample, with 80 clusters of 12
  • (D) systematic random sample
  • (E) census, because every passenger passes the barrier

4. The company suspects that satisfaction differs a great deal between first class and standard, and wants a reliable figure for each class as well as for the whole train. Which plan suits this best, and why?

  • (A) Plan 1, because surveying whole carriages is cheaper.
  • (B) Plan 2. Stratifying by class guarantees that both classes are represented in known numbers, gives a separate estimate for each class, and, because passengers are alike within a class, gives a less variable estimate for the whole train than a simple random sample of the same size.
  • (C) Plan 3, because a random start makes it the most random of the three.
  • (D) Any of the three, because all of them use randomisation.
  • (E) None of them: only a census of all 960 passengers can say anything about the train.

5. Which statement about Plan 3 is correct?

  • (A) It is a simple random sample, because every passenger has the same 1-in-12 chance of being chosen.
  • (B) It cannot give a random sample at all, because every 12th passenger is chosen.
  • (C) It is a simple random sample only if the passengers pass the barrier in a random order.
  • (D) It is a stratified sample, because each block of 12 passengers contributes one person.
  • (E) Every passenger has the same chance of being chosen, but it is not a simple random sample. Only 12 different samples are possible, so two passengers who pass the barrier one after the other can never both be chosen. In a simple random sample, every possible group of 80 is equally likely.

Questions 6–10 · Experiment or observational study?

6. Which of these studies is an experiment?

  • (A) A bakery bakes 30 batches of the same dough, randomly assigns 10 batches to each of three oven temperatures (180, 200 and 220 °C), and measures how high each loaf rises.
  • (B) A researcher compares the rise heights of loaves from three bakeries that happen to use different oven temperatures.
  • (C) A baker looks back through two years of records to see whether loaves baked on humid days rose less.
  • (D) A survey asks 400 home bakers what oven temperature they use and how often their bread fails to rise.
  • (E) A food magazine asks readers to send in photos of their loaves and records the oven temperature each reader reports.

7. For the bakery experiment in question 6, option (A), which identification is correct?

  • (A) Experimental units: the three oven temperatures. Response: the batches of dough.
  • (B) Factor: rise height. Levels: the 30 batches.
  • (C) Experimental units: the 30 batches. Factor: oven temperature, with three levels (180, 200, 220 °C), which are also the three treatments. Response: rise height.
  • (D) Experimental units: the loaves' rise heights. Treatments: the 30 batches.
  • (E) There are no treatments, because the dough is the same in every batch.

8. A researcher selects 500 people who passed their driving test this month and records how many accidents each has over the next three years. This study is

  • (A) an experiment, because the drivers were selected by the researcher
  • (B) a prospective observational study
  • (C) a retrospective observational study
  • (D) a census, because all 500 drivers are followed
  • (E) a survey, because the drivers will be asked questions

9. A study of 600 randomly selected secondary-school students finds that those who eat breakfast have higher average grades. For a variable to be a confounding variable here, it must be

  • (A) associated with grades only
  • (B) associated with eating breakfast only
  • (C) any variable measured in the study
  • (D) associated with both eating breakfast and grades, so that it offers another explanation for the association between them
  • (E) a variable that the researcher assigned to the students

10. A fitness-app company invites users to volunteer. The 1,200 volunteers are given a new daily step target, and the company compares their steps in the month before and the month after. There is no group without the new target. Which statement is correct?

  • (A) It is an observational study, because the participants volunteered.
  • (B) It is an observational study, because there is no comparison group.
  • (C) It is a census, because every volunteer took part.
  • (D) It is an experiment, and because it is an experiment, its results show that the step target causes more walking for all the app's users.
  • (E) It is an experiment, because the company imposed the new target. But the participants are volunteers, so any conclusion applies only to people like them, and with no comparison group the before-and-after change could have other causes, such as the change of season.

Questions 11–14 · The investigative question

A water company serves the town of Northbridge. It is considering a campaign to encourage water-saving shower heads.

11. Which of these is a census?

  • (A) The company surveys a random sample of 500 Northbridge households about their shower heads.
  • (B) The company reads the water meter of every one of Northbridge's 14,300 households to find the mean daily water use for the town.
  • (C) The company surveys every household on the three streets nearest its office.
  • (D) The company surveys 500 households and gets a 100% response.
  • (E) The company surveys a random sample of households from every district in the town.

12. The company writes this investigative question: "Is the proportion of all Northbridge households that have a water-saving shower head greater than 0.30?" What does its second component tell you?

  • (A) The parameter is the proportion of all Northbridge households with a water-saving shower head, and the analysis is a hypothesis test with the direction "greater than".
  • (B) The parameter is 0.30, and the analysis is an estimate.
  • (C) The variable is the number of shower heads in each house, and the analysis is a census.
  • (D) The parameter is the proportion of the households surveyed that have the shower head.
  • (E) It has no second component, because it does not name a sample size.

13. Which investigative question calls for estimating a parameter within a range of values, rather than testing a claim?

  • (A) "Is the mean daily water use of Northbridge households less than 350 litres?"
  • (B) "Do more than 30% of Northbridge households have a water meter?"
  • (C) "What is the mean daily water use, in litres, of Northbridge households, to within a plausible range of values?"
  • (D) "Should the company launch the campaign?"
  • (E) "Is there an association between household size and shower-head type in Northbridge?"

14. The company plans to survey a random sample of Northbridge households, asking only what shower head they have. What does the third component of its investigative question allow it to conclude?

  • (A) That the campaign will cause households to switch shower heads.
  • (B) Something about the 500 surveyed households only.
  • (C) Something about households in every town the company serves.
  • (D) Nothing, because a survey cannot support any conclusion.
  • (E) A conclusion about the population of all Northbridge households, because they were randomly sampled from it. Because nothing was assigned, it cannot be a cause-and-effect conclusion.

Questions 15–20 · Separate questions

15. A radio station asks listeners to phone in and say whether they support a new cycle lane. Of the 2,300 callers, 78% say yes. To whom can this result reasonably be generalised?

  • (A) All the town's residents, because 2,300 is a large sample.
  • (B) All the station's listeners, because they all had the chance to call.
  • (C) All the town's residents, because the percentage is very high.
  • (D) Only people similar to those who chose to call in. The callers volunteered themselves, so they are not a random sample of any larger population.
  • (E) No one at all, not even people like the callers.

16. Two studies of a new revision app:

Study X. A random sample of 400 of a county's 12,000 Year 11 students is asked whether they use the app, and their exam results are recorded. Users score higher on average.
Study Y. 120 volunteer students are randomly assigned to use the app or not for a term. The app group scores higher on average.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Both studies show that the app causes higher scores for all the county's Year 11 students.
  • (B) Study X can be generalised to the county's Year 11 students, but cannot show cause, because nothing was assigned. Study Y can support a cause-and-effect conclusion, but only for students similar to its volunteers.
  • (C) Study X shows cause, because its sample was chosen at random.
  • (D) Study Y can be generalised to all the county's students, because it was randomised.
  • (E) Neither study can conclude anything, because neither is a census.

17. A teacher writes the names of 25 students on slips of paper, draws one, writes it down, puts the slip back, shakes the hat and draws again, until 5 names have been recorded. Which statement is correct?

  • (A) This is sampling without replacement, because each slip is drawn only once.
  • (B) This is a census, because every slip is in the hat.
  • (C) The same student cannot be chosen twice.
  • (D) This is a systematic random sample.
  • (E) This is sampling with replacement, so the same student could be recorded more than once. A simple random sample of 5 different students would be drawn without replacement.

18. A council wants to survey residents of a housing estate of 40 apartment buildings. Every building contains a similar mix of flat sizes, ages and tenants. The council randomly chooses 5 buildings and interviews every resident in them. Why is this cluster sample a reasonable choice here?

  • (A) Because clusters always give less variable estimates than a simple random sample.
  • (B) Because every resident has the same chance of being chosen, and that makes any sample appropriate.
  • (C) Because each building mirrors the estate as a whole, a few buildings can represent it well, and interviewing everyone in 5 buildings is far cheaper than visiting residents scattered across 40.
  • (D) Because each building is a stratum, so every type of resident is guaranteed to be included.
  • (E) Because cluster sampling is a census of each building, and a census is always best.

19. Which statement describes good strata and good clusters correctly?

  • (A) Strata should be alike inside, with the individuals within a stratum similar to each other. Clusters should each look like a small version of the whole population.
  • (B) Strata and clusters should both be as different from each other as possible.
  • (C) Strata should each look like the whole population; clusters should be alike inside.
  • (D) Strata and clusters are the same thing, called different names in different books.
  • (E) Only clusters need to be chosen carefully, because every stratum is sampled anyway.

20. A student writes this report on the commuter-train survey from questions 1–5, using Plan 2. Exactly one of the five statements is incorrect.

I. The population is the 960 passengers on the 07:40 train.
II. Plan 2 is a stratified random sample, with first class and standard class as the strata.
III. Because the passengers in each class were chosen at random, the results can be generalised to all the passengers on that train.
IV. Because all 80 passengers surveyed were on the train, the survey is a census.
V. The survey cannot show that anything the company does causes satisfaction to change, because no treatment was imposed.

Which statement is incorrect?

  • (A) I
  • (B) II
  • (C) III
  • (D) IV
  • (E) V

Answers 1–5 · The commuter train

Question 1

Answer: (C)

CED 1.11.A.5: a cluster random sample divides the population into groups (clusters), randomly selects some of the groups, and includes every individual in the chosen groups. Carriages are the clusters: 3 are chosen, and everyone in them is surveyed.

  • (A) is the trap Session 6 is built around. Both methods divide the population into groups; the difference is how the groups are used. Stratified takes some from every group; cluster takes all from some groups. Here only 3 of the 12 carriages are visited, so it is cluster.
  • (B) confuses choosing groups at random with choosing individuals at random. In a simple random sample every possible set of passengers is equally likely; here, two passengers in different carriages that were not chosen can never appear together.
  • (D) mistakes numbered groups for a fixed interval.
  • (E) confuses a census of the chosen clusters with a census of the population. A census records every individual in the population (CED 1.10.B.1); 9 carriages were not visited.
Question 2

Answer: (A)

CED 1.11.A.4: a stratified random sample divides the population into groups (strata) and takes a simple random sample from each stratum. Here the strata are the two classes, and both are sampled. The allocation is proportional: 80 × 240/960 = 20 and 80 × 720/960 = 60.

  • (B) is the reverse of question 1's trap. Groups alone do not make a cluster sample; taking some from every group makes it stratified.
  • (C) is true about the chance, 20/240 = 60/720 = 1/12, and wrong about the conclusion. Equal chance for every individual is not the definition of a simple random sample (question 5).
  • (D) has no fixed interval.
  • (E) names a Session 7 idea and misapplies it. Within each class the passengers are chosen at random, so this is a random sampling method.
Question 3

Answer: (D)

CED 1.11.A.6: a systematic random sample chooses a random starting point and then takes every kth individual. Here k = 960 ÷ 80 = 12, with a random start from 1 to 12.

  • (A) mistakes a random start for a random sample of every possible group (question 5).
  • (B) and (C) invent groups the plan does not use. The blocks of 12 passengers passing the barrier are not strata or clusters: nothing is sampled within them, and none is taken whole.
  • (E) confuses who is passed through with who is surveyed. Only 80 are surveyed.
Question 4

Answer: (B)

CED 1.11.B.1: each method has characteristics that make it more appropriate depending on the question being investigated. The company wants an estimate for each class, and expects the classes to differ. That is exactly what stratifying by class delivers, and a justification names those characteristics.

  • (A) gives a genuine advantage of cluster sampling, but not the one this question needs. With 3 carriages out of 12, first class could easily be missed altogether.
  • (C) treats "random" as something one plan can have more of. All three plans are random; they differ in what they guarantee.
  • (D) is the vague justification the CED's sample scoring rejects. "Random" alone does not justify a method.
  • (E) confuses what a sample can do with what a census can do. A well-chosen random sample supports conclusions about the population it was drawn from (CED 1.10.E.2).
Question 5

Answer: (E)

CED 1.11.A.3: in a simple random sample, every group of n individuals has an equal chance of being the sample. Plan 3 gives every passenger a 1-in-12 chance, but only 12 different samples can ever occur: start at 1, 2, …, or 12. A simple random sample of 80 from 960 could be any of more than 10¹¹⁸ different groups. So two passengers who pass the barrier together can never both be chosen.

  • (A) is the designed trap: equal chance for each individual is not equal chance for each sample. Stratified samples share the same property, as question 2 shows.
  • (B) overcorrects. A systematic sample is a random sampling method; it is simply not a simple random one.
  • (C) confuses the order of the passengers with the definition. Even with a random order, only 12 samples are possible from a fixed start-and-step rule.
  • (D) invents strata.

Answers 6–10 · Experiment or observational study?

Question 6

Answer: (A)

CED 1.10.C.1: an experiment is a study in which the researcher assigns conditions, or treatments, to experimental units. Only in (A) does anyone decide which batch gets which temperature.

  • (B) compares groups that already exist. The bakeries chose their own temperatures, so this is observational, however carefully the loaves are measured.
  • (C) is a retrospective observational study: past records (CED 1.10.D.3).
  • (D) is a survey, an observational study using a standard set of questions (CED 1.10.D.4).
  • (E) is observational too, and relies on readers who chose to take part.
Question 7

Answer: (C)

CED 1.10.C.2–4:

  • the experimental unit is what the treatment is applied to: a batch
  • the factor is the explanatory variable imposed: oven temperature
  • its levels are the three temperatures, which are the treatments when there is one factor
  • the response is measured after treatment: rise height
  • (A) swaps the treatment and the units: the temperatures are applied to the batches.
  • (B) makes the response a factor.
  • (D) makes the response the unit and the units the treatments.
  • (E) confuses "the same dough" with "the same treatment". Holding the dough fixed is what lets temperature be the only thing that differs.
Question 8

Answer: (B)

CED 1.10.D.2: a prospective study selects its units at one point in time and gathers data both then and into the future. Nothing is imposed, so it is observational.

  • (A) confuses selecting the units with assigning treatments to them. Every study selects its units.
  • (C) reverses the direction. A retrospective study would look up the past accident records of drivers selected now.
  • (D) confuses following everyone in the sample with a census of the population of new drivers.
  • (E) is not the best description. Accidents can be recorded from insurance and police records without asking the drivers anything, and "prospective" is the feature the question tests.
Question 9

Answer: (D)

CED 1.10.D.5: to be a confounding variable, a variable must be associated with both the explanatory variable and the response variable. For example, household routine might go with both eating breakfast and doing homework. Because it is linked to both, it offers an alternative explanation, and the study cannot say that breakfast raises grades.

  • (A) and (B) name only one link. This is the half-credit answer the session warns about: a variable linked only to grades cannot explain why breakfast eaters have higher grades.
  • (C) confuses "measured" with "confounding". Many measured variables are unrelated to either.
  • (E) is backwards. Assigned variables are treatments, and confounding is the problem that assignment is designed to remove.
Question 10

Answer: (E)

The company imposed a treatment, the new target, so it is an experiment (CED 1.10.C.1). Two limits still apply:

  • The units were volunteers, so conclusions reach only people similar to them (CED 1.10.E.4).
  • There is no comparison group, so the before-and-after change could come from anything else that changed between the two months.

This is the session's fifth Odd One Out card: an experiment with no comparison is still an experiment, only a weak one.

  • (A) thinks volunteers make a study observational. Whether a study is an experiment depends on whether treatments were imposed, not on how the units were recruited.
  • (B) thinks a comparison group is part of the definition. It is part of a good design, which is Session 7.
  • (C) confuses taking part with a census of the app's users.
  • (D) overreaches twice: the design cannot rule out other causes, and volunteers do not represent all users.

Answers 11–14 · The investigative question

Question 11

Answer: (B)

CED 1.10.B.1: a census records information from all items or individuals in a population. Every one of the 14,300 households is metered, so the mean is a parameter, as Session 1 put it.

  • (A) is a random sample.
  • (C) is everyone on three streets, which is a census of those streets only. The population of interest is the town.
  • (D) confuses a full response from a sample with a census. 500 of 14,300 is still a sample.
  • (E) is a stratified random sample.
Question 12

Answer: (A)

CED 1.10.A.2 and 1.10.A.2.i: the second component guides the analysis. For a hypothesis test it makes clear the parameter and the direction of the alternative. Here the parameter is the proportion of all Northbridge households, and "greater than 0.30" gives the direction.

  • (B) mistakes the benchmark value for the parameter. 0.30 is the value the parameter is being compared with. The phrase "is … greater than" signals a test, not an estimate.
  • (C) changes the variable (the question asks whether a household has the shower head, not how many) and invents a census.
  • (D) names a statistic, not the parameter. The question is about all households, not the ones surveyed.
  • (E) confuses the components with details of the design. Sample size is not one of the three components.
Question 13

Answer: (C)

CED 1.10.A.2.ii: for a confidence interval, the question makes clear the parameter and the goal of estimating it within a range of potential values. (C) names the parameter, mean daily water use, and asks for a plausible range.

  • (A), (B) and (E) each test a claim, with the directions "less than", "greater than" and "associated". They call for hypothesis tests (1.10.A.2.i), which the course reaches in Sessions 14–15.
  • (D) is a decision question, not an investigative one. Session 1's test made the same point: statistics can inform a decision, but a decision is not what a study measures.
Question 14

Answer: (E)

CED 1.10.A.3: the third component gives the population the conclusions apply to and the type of conclusion. With households randomly sampled from Northbridge, the conclusions generalise to all Northbridge households (CED 1.10.E.2). Nothing is assigned, so there is no cause-and-effect conclusion.

  • (A) reaches for cause from a survey, and about a campaign that has not happened.
  • (B) undersells a random sample. Random selection is exactly what licenses reaching beyond the sample.
  • (C) overreaches to other towns that were not sampled.
  • (D) is false. Surveys support conclusions about the population sampled; what they cannot support is cause.

Answers 15–20 · Separate questions

Question 15

Answer: (D)

CED 1.10.E.3–4: a sample is not random when units deliberately choose or volunteer themselves, and then generalisations are appropriate only to individuals similar to those in the study. Callers who care strongly enough to phone are unlikely to be typical of the town.

  • (A) treats size as a substitute for randomness. A large self-selected sample is still self-selected.
  • (B) confuses having the chance with being chosen at random. The listeners chose themselves.
  • (C) treats a strong result as a representative one.
  • (E) overcorrects. The result still describes people like the callers.

Session 7 gives this problem its name. The reason it matters is already here: the sample chose itself.

Question 16

Answer: (B)

This is the distinction Session 6's Scenario B was built on:

Random selection?Random assignment?So it can…
Study Xyesnogeneralise to the county's Year 11; not show cause
Study Yno (volunteers)yesshow cause, for students like the volunteers
  • (A) merges the two. Neither study gives both.
  • (C) is the error the CED's own unit guide names: random selection does not license cause. App users may differ from non-users in many ways, such as motivation.
  • (D) is the reverse error. Random assignment does not make volunteers representative of the county.
  • (E) thinks only a census supports conclusions (question 4, option E).
Question 17

Answer: (E)

CED 1.11.A.1–2: sampling with replacement returns each selected individual to the pool before the next draw, so the same individual can be selected more than once. Putting the slip back is exactly that. A simple random sample of 5 different students would not return the slips.

  • (A) reverses the definitions. Each slip can be drawn many times.
  • (B) confuses the pool with the sample. 5 are recorded, not 25.
  • (C) is the property of sampling without replacement.
  • (D) has no interval and no fixed start.
Question 18

Answer: (C)

A cluster sample works well when each cluster looks like the whole population, so that a few clusters can stand in for it, and when visiting whole clusters is cheaper. This estate meets both conditions: every building has a similar mix.

  • (A) is false. Cluster samples are usually more variable than a simple random sample of the same size, and their advantage is cost. That is why the similar mix in each building matters here.
  • (B) is question 5's trap: equal chance for each individual does not make every design appropriate.
  • (D) confuses clusters with strata. Only 5 buildings are visited, so nothing guarantees that every type of resident is included.
  • (E) confuses a census of the chosen buildings with a census of the estate (question 1).
Question 19

Answer: (A)

The two designs want opposite things from their groups:

  • Strata should be alike inside, so that sampling a few from each gives precise estimates.
  • Clusters should each be a miniature of the population, so that taking a few whole clusters represents it.

This is the session's "which way round the groups are used", as a property of the groups themselves.

  • (B) asks for something neither design needs.
  • (C) swaps them, which is Session 6's main trap in another form.
  • (D) treats two different designs as one.
  • (E) ignores the fact that strata chosen badly, with nothing in common inside, give little benefit over a simple random sample.
Question 20

Answer: (D)

Statement IV confuses being in the population with being the whole population. A census records every individual in the population (CED 1.10.B.1). 80 of 960 is a sample.

  • (A) I is correct: the company's question is about passengers on the 07:40.
  • (B) II is correct (question 2).
  • (C) III is correct: random sampling within each stratum still licenses generalising to the population sampled (CED 1.10.E.2).
  • (E) V is correct: a survey imposes no treatment, so it supports no cause-and-effect conclusion.
