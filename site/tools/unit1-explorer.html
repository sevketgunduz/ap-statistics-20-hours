What to notice

The green line never moves — p is a fixed fact about the population, whether or not anybody measures it. Every blue dot is a statistic, and they scatter around the line rather than landing on it.

Now drag the sample size

Set n to 10 and draw 50 samples, then clear and do the same at n = 500. The dots stay centred on the same line, but the spread collapses. Bigger samples do not make you right — they make you reliably close. That single sentence is what Units 3 and 4 are built on.

The honest part

In a real study the green line is invisible. You get exactly one blue dot and no way of knowing how far from the line it landed. That is the entire problem statistics exists to solve.

On the journey times, compare width 10 with width 5. At 10 there is one obvious peak and the right skew is unmistakable. At 5 the peak splits in two and the whole thing looks flatter — almost bimodal. Then try width 2: now it is mostly noise. Neither picture is wrong.

Why the exam asks for shape, not for a picture

Because the picture is a choice. Skew, the single peak and the outlier at 68 survive every sensible bin width; the exact bar heights do not. Describe the things that survive.

Skew is named for the tail, not the bulk.

The trick that works

Put a finger on the long thin tail and say its direction out loud. That is the name. Ignore where the tall bars are — they are always on the other side.

Getting eight in a row

Anything less than a streak of eight means it is still guesswork. This takes about four minutes and saves a mark on every distribution question you meet between now and the exam.
