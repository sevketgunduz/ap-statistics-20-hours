Session 8 — Multiple-Choice Test

AP Statistics · Twenty-Hour Course · CED Topics 2.1, 2.2, 2.3 20 questions · 35 minutes · calculator allowed

Before you start

None of the scenarios here is one you worked in Session 8. The Lincoln High travel register, the hospital waiting table, the café, the library branches and the St Mary's overbooking simulation have all been left out. Each question gives you a new table, a new graph or a new random process.

The test uses the session's conventions throughout:

  • Joint relative frequency: a cell divided by the table total. Marginal: a row or column total divided by the table total. Conditional: a cell divided by the total of the row or column you are restricting to.
  • Proportions are given to 3 decimal places and percentages to 1. They are computed from the counts, never from rounded values. The condition is always named: "of the flats, …".
  • Two variables are associated when the conditional relative frequencies differ across levels of the other variable. Counts on their own are never evidence, and association is not cause.
  • In a mosaic plot, a column's width is its marginal relative frequency, a tile's height is a conditional relative frequency, and a tile's area is the joint relative frequency.
  • A simulation has a model, an assignment of random digits, a defined trial and event, many trials, and an estimate equal to the number of trials with the event divided by the number of trials.

The CED has six learning objectives for these three topics, so this test has 20 questions.

Questions 1–7 · Pets and housing

A town council surveyed a random sample of 400 households, recording the type of home and whether the household keeps a dog, a cat, or no pet. No household kept both.
HomeDogCatNo petTotal
House906090240
Flat205288160
Total110112178400

1. What proportion of the 400 households live in a house and keep a dog?

  • (A) 0.375
  • (B) 0.225
  • (C) 0.818
  • (D) 0.600
  • (E) 90

2. What proportion of the 400 households keep no pet?

  • (A) 0.375
  • (B) 0.550
  • (C) 0.220
  • (D) 0.445
  • (E) 0.46, the average of the house and flat percentages

3. Of the households that live in a flat, what proportion keep a cat?

  • (A) 0.325
  • (B) 0.464
  • (C) 0.130
  • (D) 0.450
  • (E) 0.280

4. Of the households that keep a dog, what percentage live in a house?

  • (A) 37.5%
  • (B) 27.5%
  • (C) 81.8%
  • (D) 60.0%
  • (E) 22.5%

5. Do these data show an association between type of home and pet ownership?

  • (A) No, because the same number of houses and flats, 90 and 88, keep no pet.
  • (B) No, because a two-way table cannot show association; only a scatterplot can.
  • (C) Yes, because there are more houses than flats in the sample.
  • (D) It cannot be decided, because the two rows have different totals.
  • (E) Yes. The conditional distributions of pet ownership differ: 37.5% of houses keep a dog against 12.5% of flats, and 37.5% of houses keep no pet against 55.0% of flats.

6. A councillor says: "Households in houses are more likely to have no pet than households in flats, because 90 houses have no pet and only 88 flats do." Which is the best response?

  • (A) The claim is correct, because 90 is more than 88.
  • (B) The claim is correct, but only just.
  • (C) The claim is wrong. The two groups have different totals, 240 houses and 160 flats, so the counts cannot be compared. As conditional relative frequencies, 37.5% of houses keep no pet against 55.0% of flats: a household in a flat is more likely to have no pet.
  • (D) The claim cannot be checked without knowing how many people live in each household.
  • (E) The claim is wrong, because 88 ÷ 400 is greater than 90 ÷ 400.

7. Which conclusion do these data support?

  • (A) In this town, keeping a dog is associated with living in a house. The data do not show that the type of home is the reason, because this is an observational survey and other variables, such as garden space or landlords' rules, may be involved.
  • (B) Living in a flat causes households not to keep dogs.
  • (C) Keeping a dog causes people to move into houses.
  • (D) Nothing, because an association in a table is not evidence of anything.
  • (E) Type of home and pet ownership are independent.

Questions 13–19 · The wildlife camera

A nature reserve's camera records a fox on any given night with probability 0.3, independently from night to night. The ranger wants to estimate the probability that a fox is recorded on at least 3 of the next 5 nights, and simulates it with random digits from a random number generator.

13. In this setting, which is correct?

  • (A) The random process is "at least 3 foxes"; the event is one night.
  • (B) The outcome of one night is "the probability 0.3".
  • (C) The event is the whole five nights.
  • (D) The random process is the ranger's decision to run a simulation.
  • (E) Each night is a random process with the outcomes "fox" and "no fox". The event of interest is "a fox on at least 3 of the 5 nights".

14. Which assignment of random digits correctly models one night?

  • (A) Digits 0–3 mean a fox; 4–9 mean no fox.
  • (B) Digit 0 means a fox; 1–9 mean no fox.
  • (C) Digits 1–3 mean a fox; 4–9 mean no fox; skip any 0.
  • (D) Digits 0–2 mean a fox; 3–9 mean no fox.
  • (E) Digits 0–2 mean a fox; 3–9 mean no fox; skip any digit already used in the same trial.

15. Using the assignment in question 14's correct answer, what is one trial, and when does it count as the event?

  • (A) One trial is one digit; it counts if the digit is 0, 1 or 2.
  • (B) One trial is 3 digits; it counts if all 3 are 0, 1 or 2.
  • (C) One trial is 5 digits, one per night; it counts as the event if at least 3 of the 5 digits are 0, 1 or 2.
  • (D) One trial is 5 digits; it counts if exactly 3 of them are 0, 1 or 2.
  • (E) One trial is 30 digits, because the probability is 0.3.

16. The ranger runs 20 trials of 5 digits each:

 1: 76348    2: 60094    3: 79600
 4: 71298    5: 51651    6: 60308
 7: 47881    8: 39824    9: 58684
10: 22060   11: 79844   12: 08535
13: 45212   14: 59467   15: 45708
16: 44104   17: 24660   18: 04195
19: 50038   20: 01666

What is the simulation's estimate of the probability of a fox on at least 3 of the 5 nights?

  • (A) 0.31
  • (B) 0.60
  • (C) 0.05
  • (D) 0.10
  • (E) 0.163

17. A computer runs the same simulation with 10,000 trials. According to the law of large numbers, which is true?

  • (A) The estimate from 10,000 trials will equal the true probability exactly.
  • (B) The estimate will be larger than with 20 trials, because more trials means more foxes.
  • (C) The estimate will stay at the value found with 20 trials.
  • (D) 10,000 trials is no better than 20, because each trial is random.
  • (E) As the number of independent trials increases, the relative frequency of the event gets closer and closer to a single value, the true probability. So the 10,000-trial estimate should be much closer to it than the 20-trial one.

18. The exact probability, found with rules taught in later sessions, is about 0.163. The ranger's 20 trials gave a different estimate. What is the best explanation?

  • (A) An estimate from only 20 trials varies a great deal from one run of 20 to another. The difference is chance, and running many more trials would give an estimate much closer to 0.163.
  • (B) The simulation must have been set up wrongly.
  • (C) Random number generators are not truly random.
  • (D) The true probability must be the simulation's value, not 0.163.
  • (E) Simulations cannot estimate probabilities of more than one night.

19. The camera records a fox on four nights in a row. The ranger says: "We're due a fox-free night, so tomorrow the chance of a fox is less than 0.3." Which is the best response?

  • (A) Correct: the law of large numbers says the results must balance out.
  • (B) Correct: after four foxes the probability drops to 0.1.
  • (C) Incorrect. The nights are independent, so tomorrow's probability is still 0.3. The law of large numbers describes the long run; it says nothing about making up for a short run.
  • (D) Incorrect: tomorrow's chance is now higher than 0.3, because foxes are clearly around.
  • (E) It cannot be known, because four nights is a small sample.

III. The marginal relative frequency of flats is 160 ÷ 400 = 0.400.
IV. Type of home and pet ownership are associated in these data, because the conditional distributions of pet ownership differ between houses and flats.
V. The data do not show that living in a flat causes households not to keep a dog.

Which statement is incorrect?

  • (B) II

  • (D) IV
  • (E) V

Answers 1–7 · Pets and housing

Question 1

Answer: (B)

"House and dog" is one cell divided by the whole table: 90 ÷ 400 = 0.225. That is the joint relative frequency (CED 2.2.A.1).

  • (A) 0.375 divides by the house total, 240. That is a conditional relative frequency, "of the houses".
  • (C) 0.818 divides by the dog total, 110, which conditions the other way.
  • (D) 0.600 is the marginal relative frequency of houses, 240 ÷ 400.
  • (E) is the count, not a proportion.
Question 2

Answer: (D)

A marginal relative frequency is a row or column total divided by the table total (CED 2.2.A.2): 178 ÷ 400 = 0.445.

  • (A) 0.375 is "no pet" among houses only.
  • (B) 0.550 is "no pet" among flats only.
  • (C) 0.220 is the joint "flat and no pet".
  • (E) is the trap. It averages the two conditional percentages, (37.5 + 55.0) ÷ 2 ≈ 46%, as if houses and flats were equally common. They are not: 240 against 160. The marginal is a weighted combination, and the table total gives it directly. This is the same adding-versus-averaging error from Session 1.
Question 3

Answer: (A)

"Of the flats" restricts to the Flat row, so the denominator is 160: 52 ÷ 160 = 0.325 (CED 2.2.A.3).

  • (B) 0.464 is 52 ÷ 112, "of the cat owners, how many live in flats": the other conditioning.
  • (C) 0.130 is the joint relative frequency, 52 ÷ 400.
  • (D) 0.450 is (20 + 52) ÷ 160, the proportion of flats with a dog or a cat. It is also the top edge of the cat segment in question 8's graph.
  • (E) 0.280 is the marginal proportion of cat owners, 112 ÷ 400.
Question 4

Answer: (C)

Now the condition is dog owners, so the denominator is the Dog column total, 110: 90 ÷ 110 = 0.818 (81.8%).

  • (A) 37.5% is the Session 8 Scenario A trap: it answers "of the houses, what percentage keep a dog?", 90 ÷ 240. The same cell answers two different questions, depending on what you condition on. Session 10 turns this into P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A).
  • (B) 27.5% is the marginal proportion of dog owners, 110 ÷ 400.
  • (D) 60.0% is the marginal proportion of houses, 240 ÷ 400.
  • (E) 22.5% is the joint relative frequency, 90 ÷ 400.
Question 5

Answer: (E)

CED 2.1.A.3 and 2.2.B.1: two categorical variables are associated when the conditional relative frequencies of one differ across the levels of the other.

HomeDogCatNo pet
House (240)37.5%25.0%37.5%
Flat (160)12.5%32.5%55.0%

The distributions clearly differ, so these data show an association.

  • (A) compares counts, which is question 6's trap.
  • (B) is false. Two-way tables and their graphs are exactly how association between two categorical variables is shown (CED 2.1.A.3).
  • (C) confuses the sizes of the groups with a difference in their distributions.
  • (D) is backwards. Different totals are why we convert to conditional relative frequencies, which then can be compared.
Question 6

Answer: (C)

A claim about "more likely" is a claim about proportions within each group, and the groups have different totals. As conditional relative frequencies, houses 90 ÷ 240 = 37.5% and flats 88 ÷ 160 = 55.0%. So the counts and the proportions point in opposite directions, and the proportions answer the question. This is Session 2's comparison rule, now inside a two-way table.

  • (A) and (B) compare counts across groups of different sizes.
  • (D) asks for information that is irrelevant. The claim is about households, and the table counts households.
  • (E) reaches the right verdict with the wrong reasoning. Dividing both counts by 400 gives joint relative frequencies, which still reflect the group sizes, and here they even point the wrong way: 0.220 is less than 0.225.
Question 7

Answer: (A)

The data show an association (question 5). They cannot show a cause, because nothing was assigned: this is an observational survey (Sessions 6 and 7). The claim is also limited to this town's households, which were randomly sampled, so it can be generalised to them.

  • (B) and (C) claim causation in each direction. Neither follows from a survey.
  • (D) overcorrects. An association is real evidence about the population sampled; it is simply not evidence of cause.
  • (E) contradicts the association found in question 5.

Answers 8–12 · Graphs of the same table

Question 8

Answer: (E)

In a segmented bar chart, a segment's value is its height, not the position of its top edge. The Flat cat segment runs from 12.5% to 45%, so it is 45 − 12.5 = 32.5%, matching 52 ÷ 160 in question 3.

  • (A) reads the top edge of the segment as its value, the error the session names.
  • (B) is the dog segment below it.
  • (C) is the no-pet segment.
  • (D) is the House bar's cat segment.
Question 9

Answer: (B)

In a mosaic plot, each column's width shows the marginal relative frequency of that group: 240 of 400 households are houses (0.6) and 160 are flats (0.4). The heights within each column show the conditional relative frequencies, as in the segmented bars.

  • (A) and (D) confuse widths with heights. How many pets a group keeps shows in the heights.
  • (C) and (E) treat the widths as decoration. They are the one thing a mosaic plot adds to a segmented bar chart (question 10).
Question 10

Answer: (D)

Every bar of a segmented bar chart is drawn to 100%, so it hides the group sizes. A mosaic plot shows them through the column widths (question 9). The session's "Choosing among the three graphs" table sets out what each graph shows and hides; this is the mosaic plot's advantage.

  • (A), (B) and (C) can all be read from either graph, because both show the conditional relative frequencies.
  • (E) is not in the data at all. The table records only which kind of pet a household keeps.
Question 11

Answer: (A)

Identical segmented bars mean identical conditional distributions, and that is exactly what no association means (CED 2.1.A.3). Knowing whether a household is in a house or a flat would tell you nothing about its pets. The Session 8 solo case, the library's two branches, is this situation.

  • (B) confuses identical percentages with equal group sizes. A segmented bar chart hides group sizes (question 10).
  • (C) reverses the meaning. Matching bars show that the variables are not associated.
  • (D) is unrelated.
  • (E) is false: comparing the bars is how a segmented bar chart shows association.
Question 12

Answer: (B)

A tile's area = width × height = marginal × conditional = joint relative frequency: 0.4 × 0.55 = 0.22 = 88 ÷ 400. This is the course's mosaic rule.

  • (A) is the tile's height, a conditional relative frequency.
  • (C) is 88 ÷ 178, which conditions on "no pet". The plot is not drawn that way.
  • (D) is the column's width alone.
  • (E) ignores the widths (question 9).

Answers 13–19 · The wildlife camera

Question 13

Answer: (E)

CED 2.3.A.1–3: a random process generates results determined by chance; an outcome is the result of one trial; an event is a collection of outcomes. Each night is a random process with outcomes "fox" and "no fox". The event is a collection of five-night results: those with at least 3 foxes.

  • (A) swaps the terms.
  • (B) confuses an outcome with a probability.
  • (C) confuses the event with the period of observation.
  • (D) confuses the random process being modelled with the act of simulating it.
Question 14

Answer: (D)

Three of the ten equally likely digits, 0, 1 and 2, give a probability of 3 ÷ 10 = 0.3 (CED 2.3.A.4).

  • (A) uses four digits, which gives 0.4.
  • (B) uses one digit, which gives 0.1.
  • (C) skips 0, leaving nine digits, so a fox has probability 3 ÷ 9 = 0.333, not 0.3.
  • (E) is the subtle one. Skipping repeated digits would make the nights dependent, because one night's result would change the next night's chances. The model says the nights are independent, so digits may repeat.
Question 15

Answer: (C)

A trial must copy the whole situation once: five nights, so five digits. The event is the thing asked about: at least 3 foxes.

  • (A) simulates one night, not five, so it estimates 0.3 again.
  • (B) simulates only three nights.
  • (D) changes "at least 3" to "exactly 3", leaving out 4 and 5 foxes.
  • (E) confuses the probability with the number of digits.
Question 16

Answer: (D)

Count the fox digits (0, 1, 2) in each trial. Only trial 10 (22060, with 4 foxes) and trial 13 (45212, with 3) have at least 3. So the estimate is 2 ÷ 20 = 0.10.

  • (A) 0.31 counts fox digits: 31 of the 100 digits are 0, 1 or 2. That estimates the one-night probability, 0.3, not the event. This is the session's Scenario B trap, counting patients instead of sessions, in a new form.
  • (B) 0.60 counts trials with at least 2 foxes, 12 of 20.
  • (C) 0.05 counts trials with exactly 3, only trial 13.
  • (E) is the exact probability, not what these 20 trials estimate.
Question 17

Answer: (E)

This is CED 2.3.A.7, very nearly word for word. With independent trials, the long-run relative frequency settles towards a single value, the probability. A 10,000-trial estimate is expected to be far closer to 0.163 than a 20-trial one.

  • (A) overstates the law. It says "closer and closer", not "equal".
  • (B) confuses the number of events with their relative frequency.
  • (C) assumes a small run has already found the answer.
  • (D) denies the law.
Question 18

Answer: (A)

A 20-trial estimate is highly variable: runs of 20 can easily give 0.05, 0.10, 0.20 or 0.30 when the true value is 0.163. The Overbooking Simulation tool showed the same spread. A gap of this size is exactly what chance produces, and the cure is more trials (question 17).

  • (B) assumes that a difference means an error. Question 14 confirms the set-up is sound.
  • (C) blames the generator for ordinary sampling variation.
  • (D) trusts the smaller, noisier source over the exact calculation.
  • (E) is false: the whole five-night process was simulated.
Question 19

Answer: (C)

The model says the nights are independent, so no run of results changes tomorrow's probability. The law of large numbers is about the long run: over thousands of nights, the proportion with a fox approaches 0.3. That happens because the early streak becomes a tiny part of the total, not because later nights make up for it. The session names this: the law says nothing about compensation.

  • (A) and (B) are the "due" belief: the idea that chance corrects itself.
  • (D) is the reverse belief, the "hot streak". It is just as wrong under this model.
  • (E) is too cautious. The model fixes tomorrow's probability at 0.3.
