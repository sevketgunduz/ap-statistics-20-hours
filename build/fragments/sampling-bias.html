Sampling Bias

A simulated Lincoln High: 1,842 students in three home areas, and 651 of them travel mainly by bus. So the parameter, the proportion of all 1,842 who travel by bus, is known here: 0.353. Choose a way of collecting a sample, draw it, and see its statistic, the sample proportion who travel by bus. Then draw many samples and see where each method's estimates centre.

Try this first

At a sample size of 120, press Draw 100 samples once for each method. The simple random samples scatter around the dashed line, some above and some below: that is variation from sample to sample, and it is not bias. The other three rows sit consistently to one side of the line. That is bias: a systematic error in the method, so the statistic is consistently larger, or consistently smaller, than the parameter.

Then the trap

Switch to a sample size of 480 and draw 100 of each again. Every row gets narrower. The simple random samples close in on the parameter; the biased methods close in on the wrong value. A bigger sample reduces the variation from sample to sample. It does nothing about bias.

And one more

Choose Random, phoned at 4:30 pm and set the bus users' answer rate to 90%, like everyone else. About one in ten chosen students still fails to answer, but the bias vanishes. Nonresponse biases a result when the people who do not respond differ from those who do in a way that matters for the question: here, bus users are still on the bus at 4:30.
