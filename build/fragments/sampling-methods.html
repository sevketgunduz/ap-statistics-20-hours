Sampling Methods

A simulated Lincoln High: 1,842 students in 72 tutor groups, each student coloured by home area. Choose a random sampling method and draw samples of about 120. Each sample gives a statistic, the sample mean journey time. The parameter, the mean journey time of all 1,842, stays fixed. Watch how far each method's sample means scatter around it.

Try this first

Draw one sample with each method and look at the grid each time: which students did it pick? Say the method's name from the picture before you read the caption. Then press Draw 100 samples for each method and compare the four rows. Every method is random, and every method's sample means centre on the parameter. They do not all scatter the same amount. Notice too that the systematic row piles up on a few values: on one fixed roll, a random start from 1 to 15 can produce only 15 different samples, so it is not a simple random sample.

Then this

Stratified sampling by home area gives sample means that scatter less than a simple random sample of the same size. Journey times are similar within a home area and very different between areas, and stratifying makes sure every sample has the right number from each area.

And the catch

Switch the tutor groups to Formed by home area, clear the results, and draw 100 cluster samples. Now each tutor group holds students from one area, so the clusters no longer mirror the school, and five of them can easily be all Town or all Villages. The cluster sample means spread out far more. A cluster sample works best when each cluster is like a small copy of the population.
