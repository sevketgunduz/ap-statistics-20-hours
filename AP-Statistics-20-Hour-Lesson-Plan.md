# Twenty Hours to the AP Statistics Exam

A condensed teaching plan built on the College Board **Course and Exam Description effective Fall 2026**, with topic order adjusted for teaching and every session cross-referenced to Barron's, Princeton Review, and 5 Steps to a 5.

| | |
|---|---|
| Contact time | 20 × 60 min |
| CED full course | ~80 hours (107 periods × 45 min) |
| Compression | 25% |
| CED units | 5 |
| Exam | 42 MC + 4 FRQ |

---

## Read this first

**This is a compression, not a course.** The CED paces the full course at roughly 107 class periods of 45 minutes — about 80 hours. Twenty hours is a quarter of that. The plan therefore assumes students have had some prior exposure to descriptive statistics, and it assumes homework. Twenty hours of contact time cannot also carry practice volume.

**The framework changed.** The CED in use here is the one effective Fall 2026: five units rather than nine, organised around four Statistical Practices — Formulate Questions, Collect Data, Analyze Data, Interpret Results — and examined with 42 multiple-choice questions and four equally weighted 10-point free-response questions.

**All three prep books are one framework behind.** Barron's 2025, Princeton Review 2023, and both editions of 5 Steps to a 5 still teach nine units, 40 multiple-choice questions, and six free-response questions including an investigative task that no longer exists. They remain excellent for practice problems. They will also send students into topics that have been removed from the course — which is what the "Do not teach, do not study" section is for.

---

## Time allocation — hours follow exam weight

Every unit lands inside its official CED band, so no part of the exam is starved by the compression.

| CED unit | Hours | Share of plan | CED exam weight | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit 1 — Exploring One-Variable Data and Collecting Data | 5 | 25% | 20–30% | in band |
| Unit 2 — Probability, Random Variables, Distributions | 5 | 25% | 15–25% | in band |
| Unit 3 — Inference for Categorical Data: Proportions | 5 | 25% | 15–25% | in band |
| Unit 4 — Inference for Quantitative Data: Means | 3 | 15% | 10–20% | in band |
| Unit 5 — Regression Analysis | 2 | 10% | 10–20% | in band |

**The one reordering.** The CED places regression last, as Unit 5. This plan moves it to sessions 4–5, immediately after one-variable description. Both are purely descriptive work with no inference, and all three prep books independently teach bivariate description early — Barron's as Unit 2, Princeton Review in Chapter 4, 5 Steps in Chapter 7. Everything else follows CED order.

**Inference is taught once and transferred.** 5 Steps to a 5 orders inference by procedure — all confidence intervals together, then all tests together — rather than by parameter. This plan keeps the CED's parameter-first spine so the AP Classroom Progress Checks stay aligned, but adopts the reasoning behind it: sessions 13–15 build the interval template and the test template as reusable machinery, and sessions 16 and 18–20 run openly as transfer. That is what makes three hours enough for Unit 4.

## The sequence — twenty sessions

Activities marked **[CED]** are taken from the College Board's own Sample Instructional Activities. Those marked **[Adapted]** are built in the same style to fill gaps.

### Block A — Describing one variable

*Sessions 1–3 · CED Unit 1*

#### Session 1 — Topics 1.1, 1.2, 1.3

The statistical investigation cycle established as the spine of the whole course. Population, sample, parameter, statistic. Categorical versus quantitative variables. One-way and two-way tables with relative frequencies.

**Activity — Population or Sample? [Adapted]**
Groups of four receive eight short study descriptions and label the population, sample, variable, and variable type for each. Two of the eight are deliberately ambiguous — the argument about those two is the lesson.

`B pdf 79–83` · `PR 99–104` · `5S 53–58`

#### Session 2 — Topics 1.4, 1.5, 1.6

Bar charts and pie charts. Dotplots, stemplots, histograms. The **shape–outliers–center–spread** description language, introduced as a script students will reuse for the rest of the course.

**Activity — Gallery Walk [CED]**
Groups of four each build a different display — dotplot, stemplot, histogram, boxplot — from one set of student-generated data such as minutes taken to get to school. After the walk, discuss what each display shows more easily than the others.

`B pdf 83–96` · `PR 105–124` · `5S 60–65`

#### Session 3 — Topics 1.7, 1.8, 1.9

Mean, standard deviation, quartiles, resistance. The 1.5 × IQR outlier rule. *z*-scores and percentiles. Boxplots. **Comparing** distributions — a CED task verb that demands an explicit two-sided quantitative comparison, and a perennial free-response point-loser.

**Activity — Match Mine [CED]**
Pairs sit either side of a folder with identical blank 3×3 grids and nine cards showing distributions of differing shape, center, skewness, and clustering. Student A arranges their cards and describes the arrangement; Student B reproduces it from the description alone. Precision of language is the entire scoring mechanism.

`B pdf 107–133` · `PR 124–143` · `5S 66–73`

### Block B — Describing two variables

*Sessions 4–5 · CED Unit 5*

> Moved up from the CED's final position. This is descriptive regression only — there is no inference for slope in the current course.

#### Session 4 — Topics 5.1, 5.2

Scatterplots described by direction, unusual features, form, and strength. Correlation *r*: its properties, and the two things it cannot tell you — that association is causal, or that a weak *r* means no relationship.

**Activity — Sketch and Switch [CED]**
Students sketch a scatterplot from a verbal prompt such as "weak negative, non-linear association," then swap papers and critique each other's sketch against the prompt.

`B pdf 165–174` · `PR 144–147` · `5S 93–98`

#### Session 5 — Topics 5.3, 5.4, 5.5

The least-squares line and prediction. Interpolation versus extrapolation. Residuals and residual plots as the test of whether a linear model belongs. Interpreting slope, intercept, and *r*² in context, and **reading regression output from software**.

**Activity — Reversing Interpretations [CED]**
Instead of asking students to interpret a residual, hand them the residual and the regression equation and ask what it says about that individual. "One wolf had a length of 1.4 m and a residual of −9.87. What does that tell us about that wolf?"

`B pdf 174–191` · `PR 147–167` · `5S 99–104`

---

### Block C — Collecting data

*Sessions 6–7 · CED Unit 1*

> These two sessions carry free-response Question 1 — Practices 1 and 2 — almost single-handedly. That is 12.5% of the exam.

#### Session 6 — Topics 1.10, 1.11

The investigative question revisited now that students can analyse data. Simple random, stratified, cluster, and systematic sampling. Why randomisation is what licenses inference — the idea the rest of the course rests on.

**Activity — Odd One Out [CED]**
Groups of four each get four study descriptions. Three share a type — observational or experimental — and one does not. Groups identify the outlier and justify the call.

`B pdf 220–237` · `PR 180–186` · `5S 122–125`

#### Session 7 — Topics 1.12, 1.13

Voluntary response, undercoverage, nonresponse, and response bias. Experiments versus observational studies. Control, randomisation, replication; blocking, blinding, confounding. What scope of inference a given design actually supports.

**Activity — Password-Style Game [CED]**
Nine terms — the four sampling designs plus bias, voluntary response, undercoverage, nonresponse, and response bias. One partner faces away; the other gives clues from the projected term. The pair naming the most terms wins.

`B pdf 245–262` · `PR 187–196` · `5S 126–133`

---

### Block D — Probability and distributions

*Sessions 8–12 · CED Unit 2*

#### Session 8 — Topics 2.1, 2.2, 2.3

Two-way tables: joint, marginal, and conditional percentages. Simulation as a way of estimating a probability before any rules exist. The tables built here become the concrete object that conditional probability is defined on in session 10.

**Activity — Quiz-Quiz-Trade [CED]**
Each student gets a card with a two-way-table question and its answer on the back. Students pair up, quiz each other, swap cards, and find a new partner. Keeps circulation and repetition high in a short session.

`B pdf 152–160` · `PR 167–170` · `5S 154–156`

#### Session 9 — Topics 2.4, 2.5

The law of large numbers. Basic probability rules and complements. Mutually exclusive events, and the addition rule that follows from them.

**Activity — Think-Pair-Share [CED]**
Five probability questions — one each for the complement rule, a mutually exclusive event, a conditional probability, the general multiplication rule, and an independent event. Students identify only the formula needed, with no calculation, then compare with a partner. Recognition is the skill being drilled.

`B pdf 273–277` · `PR 206–221` · `5S 143–147`

#### Session 10 — Topics 2.6, 2.7

Conditional probability, built directly on session 8's two-way tables. Independence, the general multiplication rule, and unions.

**Activity — Independent or Mutually Exclusive? [Adapted]**
Pairs sort twelve event pairs into independent, mutually exclusive, both, or neither — then answer the question that resolves the confusion permanently: why can two events with non-zero probability never be both? Drawn from 5 Steps content tip 14, which flags this as a standing exam trap.

`B pdf 276–281` · `PR 206–221` · `5S 143–147`

#### Session 11 — Topics 2.8, 2.9, 2.10

Random variables and their probability distributions. Expected value and standard deviation. Combining and transforming random variables. The binomial setting, and binomial mean and standard deviation.

**Activity — Predict and Confirm [CED]**
Before running a class simulation, students predict with a neighbour what values the distribution will take and how often — for instance a binomial setting with *p* = 0.5 and *n* = 16 over 20 trials. Then run it and compare against the prediction.

`B pdf 282–296` · `PR 222–241` · `5S 148–159, 174–178`

#### Session 12 — Topics 2.11, 2.12

The normal distribution and normal calculations. Then **sampling distributions and the Central Limit Theorem** — the hinge of the entire course. Everything before this session is description; everything after it is inference. Do not let this one get compressed.

**Activity — Reversing Interpretations [CED]**
Pairs receive four pictures of normal distributions with different regions shaded and write the question that would have produced each picture. A value of 15 labelled with shading to its right becomes "what is the probability that a value exceeds 15?"

`B pdf 305–334` · `PR 242–258` · `5S 74–79, 180–186`

---

### Block E — Inference for proportions

*Sessions 13–17 · CED Unit 3*

> Sessions 13–15 build the templates. Say so out loud to students — the structure established here is reused verbatim for the rest of the course.

#### Session 13 — Topics 3.1, 3.2, 3.3

Estimators, bias, and variability. The sampling distribution of p̂. Constructing a one-proportion *z*-interval, with conditions **shown rather than declared**.

**Activity — The Scribe and the Calculator [CED]**
Partners construct and interpret a confidence interval, but one may only use the calculator and the other may only write. Each must talk the other through what to do. Forces the verbalisation that written justification depends on.

`B pdf 337–345` · `PR 268–274, 292–301` · `5S 198–205`

#### Session 14 — Topics 3.4, 3.5, 3.6

Interpreting an interval, and interpreting the confidence level — two different sentences students routinely merge. Setting up H₀ and Hₐ about parameters, never statistics. **p-values**.

**Activity — Sentence Starters [CED]**
Given hypotheses, a p-value, a significance level, and a context, students complete a fill-in-the-blank conclusion sentence. The target error to eliminate: any wording implying evidence "accepts" the null.

`B pdf 348–353` · `PR 274–288` · `5S 208–210`

#### Session 15 — Topics 3.7, 3.8

Carrying out the one-proportion *z*-test end to end. Type I and Type II errors, **power**, and the four things that move it — sample size, standard error, true parameter distance, and significance level.

**Activity — Consequences Sort [Adapted]**
Six real scenarios — a drug trial, an airport scanner, a factory quality check and so on. Groups decide which error type is worse in each and what significance level that argues for, then defend the choice. Makes α a decision rather than a default 0.05.

`B pdf 419–426` · `PR 288–292` · `5S 210–214`

#### Session 16 — Topics 3.9–3.13

Two proportions: sampling distribution of the difference, confidence interval, and significance test with pooled standard error. Taught explicitly as **transfer** — ask what changes and what does not, rather than re-deriving.

**Activity — Error Analysis [CED]**
Pairs receive twenty sets of hypotheses, each containing a common student mistake — statistics in place of parameters, reversed inequalities, wrong parameter entirely. Students circle the fault, explain it, and write the correct version.

`B pdf 357–366` · `PR 321–325` · `5S 237–241`

#### Session 17 — Topics 3.14, 3.15

Chi-square tests for homogeneity and independence: setting up hypotheses, computing expected counts, checking conditions, and stating a conclusion in context. **Goodness-of-fit is no longer in the course** — skip it wherever the books offer it.

**Activity — Homogeneity or Independence? [Adapted]**
Eight study descriptions, sorted by which chi-square test applies. The discriminating question is how the data were collected — one sample classified on two variables, or several independent samples compared. Ties the session straight back to Block C.

`B pdf 437–447` · `PR 351–375` · `5S 284–289`

---

### Block F — Inference for means, and synthesis

*Sessions 18–20 · CED Unit 4*

> Three hours for a unit the CED paces at eighteen periods. This works only if Block E genuinely established the templates. If it did not, the honest fix is a twenty-second hour, not a reshuffle.

#### Session 18 — Topics 4.1, 4.2, 4.3

The sampling distribution of x̄. The *t*-distribution and why σ being unknown forces it. One-sample and **paired** *t*-intervals — paired data treated as a single-sample problem on the differences.

**Activity — Quick Write [CED]**
Two minutes, no notes: "Describe how to construct a sampling distribution." Target answer is a procedure — obtain a sample, collect responses, calculate the statistic, plot it, repeat. Exposes immediately who is still thinking of it as a distribution of data.

`B pdf 374–382, 400–404` · `PR 302–312` · `5S 201–206`

#### Session 19 — Topics 4.4, 4.5, 4.6

One-sample and paired *t*-tests, following the template from session 15 exactly. The sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂.

**Activity — Round Table [CED]**
Groups of four, each with an identical sheet of four inference problems. Each student completes problem one, then passes clockwise; the next student checks that work before starting problem two. Papers rotate until each returns to its owner. Checking others' conditions is where the learning sits.

`B pdf 388–392` · `PR 312–321` · `5S 230–235`

#### Session 20 — Topics 4.7–4.10 + synthesis

Two-sample *t*-intervals and tests. Then the half-hour that matters most: **choosing the right procedure** across Units 3 and 4. Barron's devotes a standalone section to this because selecting the wrong procedure zeroes an entire free-response question.

**Activity — Graphic Organizer [CED]**
Teams of two or three build a flowchart for deciding which inference procedure a given setting calls for. Questions that must appear on it: interval or test, one sample or two, mean or proportion, paired or independent, categorical with more than two categories. The finished chart is the students' revision sheet.

`B pdf 392–399, 478–484` · `PR 325–350` · `5S 235–236`

## Do not teach · do not study

**Six topics the books still cover that the exam no longer tests.**

Every prep book in this set predates the current framework. Roughly 15–20% of their combined page count is now off-syllabus. Students who revise from the contents pages will walk straight into it.

### For the teacher

| Removed topic | Why it's gone | Barron's | Princeton | 5 Steps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Inference for slopes** | Unit 5 is now descriptive regression only. No CI or *t*-test for a regression slope anywhere in the course. | Unit 9 entirely, pdf 449–476 | within Ch. 7 | Ch. 13 entirely, 258–275 |
| **Chi-square goodness-of-fit** | Only homogeneity and independence survive, as topics 3.14 and 3.15. | pdf 433–437 | within Ch. 7 | 279–284 |
| **Geometric distributions** | The binomial distribution remains as topic 2.10. Geometric does not appear in the CED at all. | pdf 296–298 | within Ch. 6 | 179–180 |
| **Transformations to achieve linearity** | Students must still recognise from a residual plot that a linear model is inappropriate — but are never asked to log-transform and refit. | pdf 192–194 | within Ch. 4 | 106–110 |
| **Influential points and leverage** | The formal vocabulary is not in the current Unit 5. Describing unusual points in a scatterplot still is. | pdf 186–191 | within Ch. 4 | 105 |
| **The Investigative Task (FRQ 6)** | The most expensive item on the list. The exam now has four equally weighted FRQs. No sixth question, no investigative task. Any book strategy about "spend 25 minutes on Part B" is obsolete. | pdf 512–535 | Part IV strategy, 77–84 | 5S20 Ch. 14 entirely |

### For students — hand this out

**If your prep book covers it and it is on this list, skip it.**

1. **Inference for the slope of a regression line.** You will interpret slope, intercept, *r*, *r*² and residuals. You will never test a hypothesis about a slope.
2. **Chi-square goodness-of-fit.** Learn homogeneity and independence. Not the third one.
3. **Geometric distributions.** Binomial only.
4. **Transformations to achieve linearity.** You need to say a linear model is a poor fit. You do not need to fix it.
5. **Influential points and leverage** as formal terms.
6. **The Investigative Task.** It does not exist. Your exam is 42 multiple-choice questions in 90 minutes, then four free-response questions in 90 minutes, each worth exactly the same.

Everything else in your book is fair game. The books remain excellent for practice problems — they are just organised around an older version of the course.

## Three threads that hold a compressed course together

None of these occupy a session of their own. All three are the difference between twenty hours that stick and twenty hours that do not.

**A five-minute warm-up, every session.** Open each of the twenty sessions with one retrieval question from a prior block — never the current one. Five minutes buys spaced retrieval, which is the first thing to collapse when a course is compressed to a quarter of its pacing.
*Source: 5 Steps 2020 Elite — "5 Minutes to a 5: 180 Daily Questions and Activities", pdf 560–979*

**Homework that interleaves.** Roughly 70% current topic, 30% cumulative. Every 5 Steps chapter ends with Cumulative Review Problems for exactly this reason, and its own six-week compressed plan is built almost entirely on rolling review. Budget about an hour of practice per session — the contact time cannot carry it.
*Source: Barron's Quizzes 1–22 · 5 Steps Cumulative Review, every chapter*

**Communication as a graded skill.** All three books converge here. Free-response answers are read by people, conditions must be shown rather than declared, and a distribution is never "normal" when you mean mound-shaped. Issue the 5 Steps tip list as a session-one handout; revisit Barron's error lists before session 20.
*Source: 5 Steps content tips, 49–50 · Princeton 77–78 · Barron's 50 Misconceptions & 50 Common Errors, pdf 536–553*
