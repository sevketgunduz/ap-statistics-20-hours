## Before you start

Every question is set in a scenario. None of them can be answered by recalling a definition — you have to apply the definition to a situation, analyse what is wrong with a claim, or put several ideas together. That is deliberate: the AP exam does not ask *"what is a parameter?"*, it hands you a study and expects you to find one.

Questions 1–22 are all on-syllabus. Question 23 is a clearly marked extension and does not count toward the total.

# The test

## Questions 1–4 · Municipal recycling

> A city's environmental department wants to know how much household recycling is produced across the city. The city contains **48,000 households**. The department selects **600 households at random** and, for each one, records:
>
> - the collection zone the household belongs to (North, South, East or West)
> - the number of recycling bins the household put out during the month
> - the total weight of the household's recycling for the month, in kilograms
> - whether anyone in the household composts food waste (yes or no)
>
> Of the 600 households studied, **216 reported composting**.

**1.** For this study, the population and its size are

- **(A)** the 600 households selected, N = 600
- **(B)** all 48,000 households in the city, N = 48,000
- **(C)** the four collection zones, N = 4
- **(D)** all households in the country, with N unknown
- **(E)** the 216 composting households, N = 216

**2.** The observational unit in this study is

- **(A)** one collection zone
- **(B)** one recycling bin
- **(C)** the one month over which data were collected
- **(D)** one kilogram of recycling
- **(E)** one household

**3.** Taking the four recorded variables in the order listed above, which classification is **entirely** correct?

- **(A)** categorical · quantitative discrete · quantitative continuous · categorical
- **(B)** categorical · quantitative continuous · quantitative continuous · categorical
- **(C)** quantitative discrete · quantitative discrete · quantitative continuous · categorical
- **(D)** categorical · quantitative discrete · quantitative discrete · quantitative discrete
- **(E)** categorical · categorical · quantitative continuous · categorical

**4.** Which of the following is a **parameter**?

- **(A)** 216, the number of studied households that compost
- **(B)** 0.36, the proportion of the 600 studied households that compost
- **(C)** 600, the number of households selected
- **(D)** the proportion of all 48,000 city households that compost
- **(E)** the mean recycling weight for the 600 studied households

## Questions 5–7 · The school library

> A school librarian has been asked to review how the library is used. She may survey students and may access the library's borrowing records.

**5.** Which of the following is the best **investigative question** for a statistical study?

- **(A)** "Is our library good enough?"
- **(B)** "How can we increase library use?"
- **(C)** "What proportion of students borrowed at least one book last term?"
- **(D)** "Why don't students read for pleasure any more?"
- **(E)** "Should the library extend its opening hours?"

**6.** After collecting her data, the librarian notices that Year 11 students borrowed far fewer books than other year groups. She finds this interesting and rewrites her report to answer *"Why do Year 11 students borrow so little?"* The most serious problem with this is that

- **(A)** the question was chosen *after* seeing the results, so a pattern that may be ordinary chance variation is being treated as an established finding
- **(B)** Year 11 is too small a group to study statistically
- **(C)** she should have surveyed students rather than using borrowing records
- **(D)** questions beginning with "why" can never be investigated statistically
- **(E)** she has not stated a population

**7.** The library recently began opening on weekday evenings, and the librarian wants to know whether this is being used. Which plan pairs a valid investigative question with data capable of answering it?

- **(A)** Ask *"Is evening opening worthwhile?"* and count the total number of visitors over the term.
- **(B)** Ask *"Why is evening opening unpopular?"* and survey students who have never visited in the evening.
- **(C)** Ask *"Do students prefer evening opening?"* and interview the five students who use it most.
- **(D)** Ask *"How many students used evening opening?"* and then decide what to measure once the first results are in.
- **(E)** Ask *"What proportion of students visit the library at least once during evening opening hours in a term?"* and record entry-card swipes by time of day for a randomly chosen sample of students.

## Questions 8–11 · The café

> A café recorded the type of every drink ordered during one week. There were **400 orders** in total.

| Drink | Orders |
|---|---|
| Espresso | 92 |
| Latte | ? |
| Tea | 76 |
| Cold brew | 60 |
| Other | 24 |
| **Total** | **400** |

**8.** The relative frequency of latte orders is

- **(A)** 0.148
- **(B)** 0.370
- **(C)** 0.230
- **(D)** 0.630
- **(E)** impossible to determine from the information given

**9.** What percentage of the week's orders were for **either** espresso **or** tea?

- **(A)** 21.0%
- **(B)** 19.0%
- **(C)** 42.0%
- **(D)** 58.0%
- **(E)** 84.0%

**10.** The café manager claims: *"Most of our orders are lattes."* Which is the best assessment of this claim?

- **(A)** Supported, because latte is the most frequently ordered drink.
- **(B)** Supported, because the latte percentage is higher than that of any other category.
- **(C)** Not supported, because the table gives counts rather than percentages.
- **(D)** Not supported, because "most" means more than half and lattes account for 37.0% of orders.
- **(E)** Impossible to assess without knowing how many individual customers visited.

**11.** Which of the following statements is **both factually correct and appropriately worded** as a conclusion from this table?

- **(A)** "More orders were for lattes than for any other single drink, at 37.0%, though fewer than two in five orders were lattes."
- **(B)** "Lattes account for the majority of orders."
- **(C)** "Lattes are twice as popular as espressos."
- **(D)** "Fewer than one in ten orders were for tea."
- **(E)** "Because 'Other' accounts for only 6% of orders, it can be dropped from the table without affecting the other percentages."

## Questions 12–14 · The football club

> A football club keeps a record for each of its **240 registered players**. For every player it stores the player's **registration number**, their **squad number**, the **number of matches** they played last season, and their **total minutes on the pitch** last season.

**12.** Which of the four recorded variables are **categorical**?

- **(A)** registration number only
- **(B)** squad number only
- **(C)** registration number, squad number, and number of matches played
- **(D)** none of them — all four are recorded as numbers
- **(E)** registration number and squad number

**13.** The club's software computes the mean of all four variables. Which means are **meaningful**, and for what reason?

- **(A)** All four, because all four are stored as numbers.
- **(B)** Matches played and minutes on the pitch, because each is a counted or measured quantity with units of measure.
- **(C)** Only minutes on the pitch, because it is the only continuous variable.
- **(D)** Only matches played, because measurements should be totalled rather than averaged.
- **(E)** None of them, because a mean can only be computed for a sample, and these are all 240 players.

**14.** The club reports that its players averaged **23.4 matches** last season. A player argues that this proves *matches played* is a continuous variable, since 23.4 is not a whole number. The best response is:

- **(A)** The player is correct — any variable whose average is not a whole number is continuous.
- **(B)** The player is incorrect — *matches played* is categorical, because it labels a player's level of participation.
- **(C)** The player is correct — averaging converts a discrete variable into a continuous one.
- **(D)** The player is incorrect — *matches played* is discrete, because the values the variable itself can take are whole numbers; a mean is a summary of the data, not a value the variable can take.
- **(E)** The player is incorrect — the mean should have been reported as 23, and the need to round proves the variable is discrete.

## Questions 15–17 · The school nurse

> A school nurse measures the height of **every one of the 412 students** at her school. The mean of her measurements is **163.8 cm**.

**15.** The school newsletter reports: *"The mean height of students at our school is 163.8 cm."* In this use, 163.8 cm is

- **(A)** a parameter, because every member of the population of interest was measured
- **(B)** a statistic, because it was calculated from data that were actually measured
- **(C)** a statistic, because it is a mean
- **(D)** neither, because no sampling took place
- **(E)** a parameter only if the nurse also measured students at other schools

**16.** A district researcher uses the same 412 measurements to estimate the mean height of all **9,800 students in the district**. In *this* use, 163.8 cm is

- **(A)** still a parameter, because the number itself has not changed
- **(B)** a parameter for the school and a statistic for the district, which is a contradiction that must be resolved
- **(C)** a statistic, because the 412 students now function as a sample drawn from the district population
- **(D)** neither, because the 412 students were not randomly selected from the district
- **(E)** a statistic, because 412 is much smaller than 9,800

**17.** Questions 15 and 16 taken together demonstrate that

- **(A)** the same number may be described differently depending on who is reporting it
- **(B)** the 412 measurements should not be used for district-level work under any circumstances
- **(C)** a mean is a parameter when the number of individuals is large and a statistic when it is small
- **(D)** a census always yields parameters and a sample always yields statistics, so one of the two reports must be mistaken
- **(E)** whether a number is a parameter or a statistic depends on the population named by the investigative question, not on how the number was calculated

| Faculty | Last year | This year |
|---|---|---|

**18.** Which faculty's **share** of total sales fell by the largest amount between the two years?

- **(A)** Science
- **(B)** Arts
- **(C)** Business
- **(D)** Law
- **(E)** None — every faculty sold more books this year than last

**19.** A manager writes: *"Science is our strongest faculty and it is getting stronger — sales rose from 180 to 272."* Which is the best critique?

- **(A)** The claim is sound; 272 is larger than 180.
- **(B)** The claim cannot be assessed without knowing the prices of the textbooks.
- **(C)** The claim is wrong because Business grew by more books than Science did.

- **(C)** Whether the number of students enrolled in each faculty changed between the two years
- **(D)** The names of the students who bought the books

# Answers and explanations

Each explanation says why the key is right **and what misconception each wrong option represents.** Use the second part: a wrong answer tells you which idea to reteach, and the diagnostic table at the end maps every distractor to a specific gap.

> **Answers are collapsed.** Click a question to open it, or press **`e`** to open every explanation at once. That keeps the key hidden while the test is on a shared screen.

## Answers 1–4 · Municipal recycling

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 1</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**


The population is decided by the **investigative question** — the department wants to know about recycling *across the city*, so all 48,000 households are the individuals of interest.

- **(A)** confuses the sample with the population. The 600 are who was *measured*; the 48,000 are who the study is *about*. If the 600 were the population there would be nothing left to estimate and no reason to sample.
- **(C)** treats a *category of a variable* as the population. Zones are values the variable "collection zone" takes, not the individuals being studied.
- **(D)** over-extends the population beyond what the question asks. Nothing in the study concerns households in other cities.
- **(E)** selects a subgroup defined by the response. Whether a household composts is an outcome, not a criterion for membership in the population.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 2</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**


The observational unit is the individual each datum is collected *from* — one row of the data set. Every recorded value belongs to one household.

- **(A)** and **(D)** confuse a unit with a *value of a variable*. A zone is a category; a kilogram is a unit of measurement.
- **(B)** is the commonest error on this type of question: it picks the thing being counted rather than the thing being measured. The data are about households, some of which happen to have bins.
- **(C)** confuses the observational unit with the study period.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 3</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**


| Variable | Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Collection zone | Categorical | Values are group labels |
| Number of bins | Quantitative, **discrete** | A count — 2 or 3 bins, never 2.4 |
| Weight in kg | Quantitative, **continuous** | Measured; 14.72 kg is possible |
| Composts (yes/no) | Categorical | Two group labels |

- **(B)** calls number of bins continuous — the count/measure distinction has not landed.
- **(C)** calls collection zone quantitative, which would mean averaging zone names.
- **(D)** calls a yes/no answer quantitative, and weight discrete. Two errors.
- **(E)** calls number of bins categorical. Bins are counted and the count has units, so it is quantitative.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 4</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**


A parameter is a numerical summary of a variable **for the population**, and it is unknown here because only 600 of 48,000 households were asked.

- **(A)**, **(B)** and **(E)** are all **statistics** — every one is computed from the 600 measured households. Check the denominator: if it is 600, the number describes the sample.
- **(C)** is the subtle one. 600 is known exactly, but it is not a parameter *or* a statistic: it is the **sample size**, a feature of the study design, not a numerical summary of any variable. Students who answer (C) usually think "parameter = a known number about the study." It is not — it is a summary of a variable for the population.

</details>

## Answers 5–7 · The school library

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 5</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**


It names a **measurable variable** (whether a student borrowed at least one book), specifies a population and a time frame, and can be answered from records she already has.

- **(A)** "good enough" is undefined. No data collection could settle it, because no one has said what would count as good enough.
- **(B)** and **(E)** are **action or decision questions**, not investigative questions. They ask what to *do*. A statistical study can inform them, but it cannot answer them.
- **(D)** **presupposes its own conclusion** — that students have stopped reading for pleasure. That is a claim requiring evidence, not a starting point. It also asks "why", which needs a designed comparison rather than a survey.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 6</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**


This is CED requirement **1.1.B.1**: an investigative question should not be changed on the basis of the data analysis or results. Choosing the question *because* a pattern caught your eye means you have selected the most surprising-looking feature of the data and then treated your surprise as a finding. Had she looked at the other year groups, or the other variables, she would probably have found some other odd-looking pattern.

- **(B)** is plausible but not the *most serious* problem, and it may not even be true — a year group could be large. The fatal flaw is the ordering of question and data, not the group size.
- **(C)** is irrelevant. Borrowing records are perfectly good data for a question about borrowing.
- **(D)** is too strong. "Why" questions are investigable — with an appropriate design. The problem is the after-the-fact selection.
- **(E)** identifies a genuine omission but a minor one; the population is implicitly the school's students.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 7</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**


Both halves work. The question names a measurable variable (visiting during evening hours at least once), the population is students, and entry-card swipes by time of day for a random sample will actually answer it.

- **(A)** pairs an undefined question ("worthwhile") with data that cannot address it — a total visitor count says nothing about what proportion of students that represents.
- **(C)** takes the five heaviest users, which cannot tell you anything about students generally. It is the extreme case of a biased sample.
- **(D)** explicitly decides what to measure *after* seeing results — the same fault as question 6.
- **(B)** presupposes that evening opening is unpopular, and then surveys only students who never attend — guaranteeing that the answer confirms the presupposition.

</details>

## Answers 8–11 · The café

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 8</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**


Latte = 400 − (92 + 76 + 60 + 24) = 400 − 252 = **148**, so rf = 148 ÷ 400 = **0.370**.

- **(A)** 0.148 reads the *count* as though it were already a proportion — no division by n.
- **(C)** 0.230 is espresso's relative frequency. A wrong-row error.
- **(D)** 0.630 is 252 ÷ 400, the combined share of everything that is *not* a latte.
- **(E)** the missing count is fully determined, because relative frequencies must sum to 1 and the total is given.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 9</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**


(92 + 76) ÷ 400 = 168 ÷ 400 = 0.420 = **42.0%**. Equivalently 23.0% + 19.0%.

- **(A)** 21.0% is the **average** of 23.0% and 19.0%. This is the single most common arithmetic error with percentage tables: percentages of the same total are **added**, not averaged.
- **(B)** 19.0% is tea alone.
- **(D)** 58.0% is the complement — everything that is neither espresso nor tea.
- **(E)** 84.0% doubles the correct answer.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 10</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**


"Most" means more than half. Lattes are 37.0%, which is the **largest single share** but not a majority. This is the plurality-versus-majority distinction, and it recurs in every unit of the course.

- **(A)** and **(B)** both give a true fact — latte is the largest category — and then attach the wrong word to it. Largest is not the same as most.
- **(C)** is false: percentages are directly obtainable, since the total is given.
- **(E)** confuses **orders** with **customers**. The claim is about orders, the table counts orders, so it can be assessed. One customer may place several orders, but that does not affect a claim about the composition of orders.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 11</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**


True (37.0% is the largest share) and properly hedged — it states what the number does establish and what it does not, in context.

- **(B)** asserts a majority. False.
- **(C)** 37.0 ÷ 23.0 ≈ 1.6, not 2. Also, "popular" is doing undefined work.
- **(D)** tea is 19.0%, which is nearly one in five, not fewer than one in ten.
- **(E)** is the most instructive wrong answer. Dropping "Other" reduces the total from 400 to 376, so **every** remaining relative frequency changes — lattes would become 148 ÷ 376 = 39.4%. Relative frequency always depends on *n*.

</details>

## Answers 12–14 · The football club

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 12</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**


Both registration number and squad number are written with digits, but both are **labels**. Neither has units of measure, and arithmetic on either is meaningless — the average squad number corresponds to nothing.

- **(A)** and **(B)** each catch one of the two and miss the other. A partially-applied digits test.
- **(D)** is the raw trap: being recorded as a number does not make a variable quantitative.
- **(C)** over-applies the rule to *matches played*, which genuinely is a counted quantity with units.

**The test to apply:** does it have units of measure, and does arithmetic on it mean anything? If no to both, it is categorical.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 13</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**


A mean is meaningful when the values are quantities that can sensibly be totalled and divided. Matches played (a count, in matches) and minutes on the pitch (a measurement, in minutes) both qualify. Registration and squad numbers do not — averaging labels produces a number that refers to nothing, and may not even be a real squad number.

- **(A)** applies arithmetic wherever digits appear.
- **(C)** wrongly implies that only *continuous* variables can be averaged. Discrete counts can absolutely be averaged; see question 14.
- **(D)** inverts that error.
- **(E)** confuses the **meaningfulness** of a mean with its **classification**. The 240 players are the whole population, so these means are parameters rather than statistics — but they are entirely meaningful. Parameters are means too.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 14</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**


Discrete and continuous describe the values the **variable** can take, not the values its **summaries** can take. No player played 23.4 matches; 23.4 is a property of the data set, not of any individual. *Matches played* remains discrete.

- **(A)** and **(C)** both accept the player's reasoning. This is worth reteaching properly, because the same confusion returns in Unit 2 with discrete random variables, whose expected values are routinely non-integers.
- **(B)** reclassifies a count as categorical, which would make the mean meaningless — contradicting the reported figure.
- **(E)** reaches the right conclusion by invalid reasoning. A mean should **not** be rounded to a whole number just because the variable is whole-numbered; rounding would discard real information. Right answer, wrong justification — and on a free-response question the justification is where the marks are.

</details>

## Answers 15–17 · The school nurse

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 15</strong></summary>

**Answer: (A)**


The newsletter's population of interest is *students at our school*, and the nurse measured all 412 of them. That is a **census**, so her mean summarises the entire population: a parameter.

- **(C)** assumes that a mean is automatically a statistic. Any summary can be either; what decides it is whether you measured everybody.
- **(B)** gives the wrong criterion. Statistics are not "numbers you calculated from real data" — parameters can also be calculated, when a census is possible.
- **(D)** is half-right in noticing there was no sampling, and then draws the wrong conclusion. No sampling means the figure is a parameter, not that it is neither.
- **(E)** inverts the logic. Measuring other schools would *enlarge* the population and turn these 412 into a sample.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 16</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**


Same number, different question. The district researcher's population is all 9,800 district students, and the 412 are a subset of it — so 163.8 cm now summarises a sample and is a statistic.

- **(A)** treats the classification as a property of the number. It is a property of the **relationship** between the data and the question.
- **(B)** correctly describes the situation and then wrongly calls it a contradiction. There is nothing to resolve: two different investigative questions define two different populations.
- **(D)** raises a real concern — students at one school are unlikely to represent the district well — but that is a question of whether the sample is any *good*, not of whether it is a sample. A biased sample is still a sample, and its summary is still a statistic. Sampling quality is Session 6.
- **(E)** is right that 412 < 9,800 but that is not the reason. A sample of 9,799 would still be a sample.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 17</strong></summary>

**Answer: (E)**


This is the generalisation the pair of questions was built to produce, and it is the single most important idea in Session 1.

- **(A)** is nearly right but locates the difference in the *reporter* rather than in the *question*. Two people asking the same question get the same classification, whoever they are.
- **(C)** invents a size threshold. None exists.
- **(D)** states a true rule and then concludes wrongly. Both reports are correct, because each performs a census or a sample *relative to its own population*.
- **(B)** is an overreaction. The measurements are usable; they simply need to be described as a sample, with the representativeness concern acknowledged.

</details>

## Answers 18–20 · The university bookshop

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 18</strong></summary>

**Answer: (B)**


| Faculty | Last year | This year | Change in share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Science | 36.0% | 34.0% | −2.0 pp |
| **Arts** | **29.0%** | **26.0%** | **−3.0 pp** |
| Business | 22.0% | 25.0% | +3.0 pp |
| Law | 13.0% | 15.0% | +2.0 pp |

Arts fell by 3.0 percentage points, the largest decline.

- **(A)** Science fell, but by 2.0 pp — the second-largest fall. A plausible near-miss.
- **(C)** and **(D)** both *rose* in share.
- **(E)** is the designed trap, and the reason this scenario exists. **Every faculty sold more books**, so anyone reasoning from raw counts concludes nothing fell. But the total rose from 500 to 800 — by 60% — so a faculty can sell more books and still lose share. Counts and shares can move in opposite directions.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Question 19</strong></summary>

**Answer: (D)**


Both halves of the critique matter: Science *is* still the largest category, so the manager is not simply wrong; but "getting stronger" is a claim about relative standing, and Science's share fell. Naming what the data *do* support before naming what they do not is exactly the form the exam rewards.

- **(A)** reasons from counts alone while the totals differ.
- **(C)** is **factually false** — check it: Science rose by 92 books (180 → 272), Business by 90 (110 → 200). Science grew by more books than any other faculty. This option punishes assuming rather than subtracting.
- **(B)** raises something irrelevant. The claim is about sales volume, which the table gives.

<summary><strong>Question 20</strong></summary>

**Answer: (C)**


Enrolment is the missing denominator. If Science enrolment grew by 60% while Arts stayed flat, the share figures mean something quite different from the case where both were unchanged. Sales shares tell you about the **composition of sales**; enrolment is needed before you can say anything about how likely a student is to buy a textbook.

- **(A)** would be needed for a claim about revenue. The claim here is about numbers of books.
- **(B)** is mildly relevant to stocking decisions but does not affect the comparison of sales shares.
- **(D)** is irrelevant, and identifying individuals is precisely what a statistical summary avoids.
- **(E)** is the sophisticated trap. Relative frequencies do make the two years comparable **to each other as shares of sales** — that was question 18's lesson. But "fair" has limits: shares of sales still cannot tell you about buying behaviour per student without knowing how many students there were. Getting question 18 right and question 20 wrong is a good sign — it means the first idea has landed and the second is the next one to build.

The organisation wants the proportion for all 32 million registered voters. That is a population summary, and after surveying 2,400 people it is still unknown — the survey produces an estimate of it, not the thing itself.
